# Chapter 22 — Anticoagulants and Antiplatelet Medicines

Blood thinners are among the medicines most often involved in surgical errors: stopped too early, stopped too late, not restarted, or stopped without telling the cardiologist after a recent stent. Patients are often confused about which of their tablets are blood thinners and receive conflicting advice from different clinicians. The decision always balances two risks, bleeding from the operation and clotting if the medicine is stopped, and it must be made with the prescribing specialist and written down clearly for the patient. This chapter gives the principles and the usual timings; the hospital protocol and specialist advice take precedence.

**STEP 1 Balancing the risks and making the plan**

## 22.1 Two risks to balance

Every plan balances the bleeding risk of the procedure against the clotting risk of the patient (Figure 22.1) [1]. Minimal-bleeding procedures, such as skin surgery, dental work, and cataract surgery, can usually be performed without stopping blood thinners. For most other procedures, the medicine is paused for a set time and restarted when bleeding is controlled. Bridging with short-acting heparin injections is reserved for patients at very high clotting risk, such as those with a mechanical mitral valve or a stroke or clot within the previous three months. In patients with atrial fibrillation, bridging during warfarin interruption did not reduce strokes or clots but doubled major bleeding in a large trial [2].

## 22.2 Warfarin

Warfarin is usually stopped 5 days before surgery, with the INR checked the day before or on the morning of surgery, and restarted 12 to 24 hours afterward when bleeding is controlled [1]. Bridging is needed only for patients at very high clotting risk. For urgent surgery, warfarin can be reversed with vitamin K and, if needed, a prothrombin complex concentrate.

## 22.3 Direct oral anticoagulants

Apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, and dabigatran act quickly and wear off within days, so bridging is not needed. In the PAUSE study, a standardized plan without bridging, based on the bleeding risk of the procedure and kidney function, was associated with low rates of bleeding and stroke [3]. Table 22.2 summarizes the usual timings, and Figure 22.2 shows an example calendar that can be adapted for each patient.

**Table 22.2.** _Usual timing for interrupting direct oral anticoagulants, based on the PAUSE study and the 2022 ACCP guideline._

## 22.4 Antiplatelet medicines

Aspirin taken for heart or stroke prevention is often continued through surgery, except for procedures where even small bleeding is dangerous [1]. When a second antiplatelet medicine must be stopped, the usual intervals are about 5 days for clopidogrel, 3 to 5 days for ticagrelor, and 7 days for prasugrel [1]. Patients with coronary stents must never have antiplatelet medicines stopped without the cardiologist, and elective surgery should be timed as described in Chapter 20 [4].

**STEP 2 Restarting, recovery, and Ramadan**

## 22.5 After surgery

- **Restart** according to the written plan, once bleeding is controlled. Record the restart date on the discharge letter.

<!-- -->

- **Clot prevention.** Until the usual blood thinner is restarted, preventive heparin injections may be used according to the clot-risk assessment.

- **Spinal and epidural catheters** need specific timing for blood thinners; follow the anesthesia team’s instructions [5].

- **Discharge.** Make sure the patient knows the exact day to restart and has enough medicine.

## 22.6 Ramadan

Many patients taking anticoagulants can fast, with the timing of doses moved to iftar or suhoor. Warfarin needs closer INR monitoring because diet changes during Ramadan, and twice-daily medicines need a plan. Patients should not fast while recovering from surgery and should agree any changes with their prescriber.

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

**“Which of my tablets are blood thinners?”**

Bring all your medicines. Common blood thinners include warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, dabigatran, aspirin, clopidogrel, ticagrelor, and prasugrel. Some herbal remedies also affect bleeding.

**“Can I keep taking my blood thinner for this small procedure?”**

For many small procedures, such as skin surgery or dental work, yes. We will tell you.

**“Do I need injections while my tablet is stopped?”**

Most people do not. Injections are only for people at very high risk of clots, such as those with certain heart valves [1, 2].

**“I have a stent. Can I stop my clopidogrel?”**

Not without your cardiologist. Stopping it too soon after a stent can cause a heart attack.

**“When do I restart my blood thinner?”**

On the date written in your plan, usually 1 to 3 days after the operation, depending on the bleeding risk.

**“What if I forget and take a dose by mistake?”**

Tell us before the operation. We may need to delay it or check a blood test.

**“Can I fast in Ramadan on my blood thinner?”**

Often yes, with your doses re-timed and, for warfarin, more blood tests. Not while recovering from surgery. Ask your prescriber first.

**Golden rules**

- **Do not stop or restart any blood thinner on your own.** Follow this plan.

- **If you have a heart stent, never stop aspirin, clopidogrel, ticagrelor, or prasugrel without your cardiologist.**

- Tell us about all medicines and herbal remedies.

- If you take a dose by mistake, tell us before the operation.

**After your operation**

- Restart on the date in your plan.

- Small bruises around the wound are normal.

- Walk often to prevent clots.

**Ramadan**

- Do not fast while recovering from surgery.

- Ask your prescriber before fasting. Doses may need re-timing.
