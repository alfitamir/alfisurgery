# Chapter 07 — Common Anal Conditions: Hemorrhoids, Anal Fissure, and Perianal Abscess and Fistula

Anal conditions are among the most common reasons for a surgical clinic visit, and among the hardest for patients to talk about. Many arrive after months of embarrassment, self-treatment, or advice from family, and some fear that every bleed or lump is cancer. The three conditions in this chapter differ in cause and treatment, but the counseling shares the same foundations: privacy and dignity during the examination, a clear explanation of what is wrong, honest discussion of a stepped treatment plan, and practical advice on bowel habits, pain, and wound care.

**STEP 1 Explaining the conditions and the treatments**

## 7.1 The consultation for anal symptoms

Begin by acknowledging that the subject is difficult to discuss, and give the patient time to describe the symptoms in their own words: bleeding, pain, a lump, itching, discharge, or a change in bowel habit. Ask about stool consistency, straining, time spent on the toilet, and how long the problem has lasted. Explain the examination before it starts, offer a chaperone to every patient, and respect a request for an examiner of the same sex where it can be met. Keep the patient covered except for the area being examined.

Bleeding should not be attributed to hemorrhoids without an examination. Hemorrhoids are common and often coexist with other conditions, and bleeding from a bowel cancer can be mistaken for hemorrhoids. Anoscopy is advised for new patients whenever possible, and colonoscopy is arranged when the history or age suggests another cause, such as a change in bowel habit, anemia, weight loss, a family history of bowel cancer, or an age at which screening is due [1, 2].

## 7.2 Hemorrhoids

Dietary and lifestyle measures are the first treatment for symptomatic hemorrhoids: more fiber, enough fluid, and less straining and time on the toilet [2]. Topical preparations can relieve symptoms, but the evidence for them is limited, and steroid creams should not be used for more than two weeks [2]. When symptoms continue, rubber band ligation is the most effective office treatment for grade 1 to 3 internal hemorrhoids and is well tolerated; excisional hemorrhoidectomy is more effective but more painful, with more complications, and is reserved for grade 4 hemorrhoids, combined internal and external disease, or failure of office treatment [1]. Doppler-guided hemorrhoidal artery ligation causes less pain than excision and is an option for internal hemorrhoids [1].

Hemorrhoids are common in pregnancy and are usually treated conservatively, with banding considered after delivery if symptoms persist [2]. In patients with cirrhosis, rectal varices must be distinguished from hemorrhoids before any procedure [2]. A thrombosed external hemorrhoid usually settles over 1 to 2 weeks; selected patients seen early with severe pain benefit from excision [1].

## 7.3 Anal fissure

An anal fissure is a tear in the lining of the anal canal, usually at the back, caused by passing a hard stool. The pain causes spasm of the internal sphincter, which reduces blood flow and stops the tear from healing. Acute fissures often heal with fiber, stool softeners, and warm-water baths. For chronic fissures, topical calcium channel blockers and topical nitrates each heal about half; calcium channel blockers cause fewer side effects, especially headache, and can be used first [3]. Botulinum toxin injection is about as effective as topical therapy and causes temporary leakage in about 5 in 100 patients [3]. Lateral internal sphincterotomy is the most effective treatment, healing at least 88 in 100 fissures, with a small risk of reduced control, which is lower when the division is limited to the length of the fissure [3].

## 7.4 Perianal abscess and fistula

An anal abscess is a collection of pus, usually starting in a small gland inside the anal canal. It is treated promptly by incision and drainage; antibiotics are reserved for patients with surrounding cellulitis, signs of systemic infection, or reduced immunity, and are not an alternative to drainage [4]. After drainage, about a third to a half of patients develop a fistula, a small tunnel between the inside of the anal canal and the skin, which keeps discharging or causes repeated abscesses [4].

A simple fistula that crosses little of the sphincter muscle can be treated by opening the tract (fistulotomy), which has a high success rate. When the tract crosses more muscle, cutting it would risk continence, so a soft thread (seton) is placed to keep it draining, and a sphincter-sparing repair, such as an advancement flap or ligation of the tract, is planned later [4]. Recurrent or complex fistulas raise the possibility of Crohn’s disease.

**STEP 2 Preparation, medicines, and recovery**

## 7.5 Before the procedure

- **Bowels.** Start fiber and plenty of fluid before any procedure, and treat constipation. Some units give a small enema before surgery.

<!-- -->

- **Blood thinners.** Banding and surgery can bleed, especially when the band falls off. Plan anticoagulant and antiplatelet medicines individually (Chapter 22).

- **Anesthesia.** Banding needs no anesthetic. Surgery is done under general, spinal, or local anesthesia, usually as a day case.

- **Medicines for erectile dysfunction.** Record them before prescribing nitrate ointment.

- **Privacy and timing.** Many patients prefer to avoid the days before family events or travel. Agree a date that allows a quiet first week after surgery.

## 7.6 After the procedure

Pain after hemorrhoidectomy and fistula surgery is greatest during bowel movements in the first 7 to 10 days. Prepare the patient with a plan: regular paracetamol, an anti-inflammatory medicine if suitable, short courses of stronger medicine only if needed, and a stool softener from the first day, because opioids cause constipation and a hard stool is the most painful event of recovery. Warm-water baths or washing after each bowel movement soothe the area and keep wounds clean; patients who use water for washing after the toilet can continue to do so gently. Difficulty passing urine is more common after anal surgery and is the most frequent reason for an unplanned stay.

Open wounds after abscess drainage or fistulotomy heal from the base over several weeks. Explain that discharge and slight bleeding on the pad are expected, and that packing, if used, is changed by a nurse or reduced over time. A seton is left in place, may discharge a little, and should not be pulled.

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

**“Is the bleeding cancer?”**

Most bleeding from the back passage comes from hemorrhoids or a fissure, but we never assume that. I will examine you, and if your age or symptoms suggest it, we will look at the whole bowel with a camera test [1].

**“Will the piles come back after banding?”**

They can, especially if constipation and straining continue. Banding can be repeated. Soft stools and short toilet visits are the best protection [2].

**“Which creams should I use?”**

Creams may ease symptoms but do not cure piles. Do not use steroid creams for more than two weeks, because they thin the skin [2].

**“Why is the operation so painful?”**

The skin around the anus has many nerves. The pain is worst when opening the bowels in the first week to ten days. Soft stools, regular pain relief, and warm-water baths make a large difference.

**“Will I lose control of my bowels?”**

For most people, no. Fissure surgery carries a small risk of reduced control, mainly of wind; for fistulas, we choose an operation that protects the muscle. We will discuss your own risk before any operation [3, 4].

**“The ointment gives me a headache. What can I do?”**

Headache is common with nitrate ointment and often eases after the first days. Using less, or switching to a different ointment, usually helps [3].

**“Why can’t you just give me antibiotics for the abscess?”**

Antibiotics cannot reach pus that is trapped. The abscess must be opened and drained; antibiotics are added only in some cases [4].

**“Why do I need a thread (seton) instead of a cure now?”**

Because the tunnel passes through the muscle that controls your bowels. The thread keeps it draining safely while we plan an operation that protects that muscle.

**“Can I wash with water after the toilet?”**

Yes. Gentle washing with warm water is better than rubbing with paper, and it helps healing. Pat the area dry.

**“Can I fast in Ramadan with this condition?”**

Fasting can make stools harder. Drink plenty of water between iftar and suhoor, eat fiber, and take your softener. After surgery, wait until your bowels are comfortable before fasting.

**“I am pregnant and have piles. What can I do?”**

Piles are very common in pregnancy and usually improve after delivery. Fiber, fluids, and gentle creams are safe; procedures are usually delayed until after the baby is born [2].

**Your most important job: soft, easy stools**

- Eat fiber every day: vegetables, fruit, whole grains, beans, or a fiber supplement.

- Drink plenty of water, about 8 glasses a day unless we told you otherwise.

- Take the stool softener we gave you every day while you take strong pain medicine.

- Go when you feel the urge. Do not strain, and do not sit on the toilet for more than a few minutes. Leave your phone outside.

**1. Pain and pain medicines**

- The area will be sore, most of all when you open your bowels. This gets better day by day.

- Take your pain medicines regularly at first, as we told you.

- Sit in a shallow bath of warm water for 10 to 15 minutes, 2 or 3 times a day and after bowel movements. It eases pain and spasm.

- Strong pain medicines cause constipation. Keep taking your softener.

**2. Wound care and washing**

- Wash gently with warm water after each bowel movement and pat dry. Do not rub.

- Some blood and discharge on your underwear is normal for a few weeks. Wear a pad.

- If you have a seton, it may leak a little. Do not pull it. Keep the area clean.

- Open wounds heal from the bottom up. This takes a few weeks.

**3. Eating and drinking**

- Eat normally, with plenty of fiber and water.

- In Ramadan, drink plenty of water between iftar and suhoor and keep taking fiber. After an operation, ask us before fasting.

**4. Rest, sitting, and clothes**

- Rest for the first few days, but walk around the house.

- A soft cushion helps when sitting, praying, or travelling.

- Wear loose cotton underwear.

**5. Getting back to normal**

These are usual times. Your own plan is on your card.
