# Chapter 15 — Minor Outpatient Procedures under Local Anesthesia

Removal of lumps and skin lesions, treatment of ingrowing toenails, and drainage of skin abscesses make up a large share of outpatient surgery. They are often delegated to the most junior member of the team and fitted into a busy list, yet for the patient they are real operations, performed while awake, on a visible part of the body, with a scar that may last for life. The principles of Part I apply in full: a proper consent discussion, attention to the scar and to the pathology result, and clear aftercare. This chapter also covers the red flags that mean a lump or lesion should not be treated as minor.

**STEP 1 Explaining the condition and the procedure**

## 15.1 A minor procedure is not minor to the patient

The consent discussion for a minor procedure covers the same ground as for a larger operation (Chapter 1): what will be done, the alternatives (including leaving the lump alone), the scar, the chance of recurrence, infection and bleeding, and how the pathology result will be given. Many patients worry most about pain during the injection and about the scar on a visible area. Written consent, a chaperone for intimate areas, and a photograph where appropriate protect both patient and surgeon. Cosmetic requests are legitimate, but patients should understand that every excision leaves a scar, which may be more noticeable than the original lump.

## 15.2 Red flags: when a lump is not minor

Most subcutaneous lumps are lipomas or cysts, but a soft tissue sarcoma can look similar. An unexplained lump that is increasing in size should be imaged with urgent ultrasound, and features suggesting sarcoma need referral to a specialist service [1]. Lumps larger than 5 cm, deep to the muscle layer, growing, painful, or recurring after removal should not be shelled out in a minor procedures list (Figure 15.1). Pigmented lesions that are changing, irregular, bleeding, or new in an adult should be referred to dermatology or removed completely with a narrow margin; they should never be shaved or partly removed.

## 15.3 Lipomas, cysts, and skin lesions

A lipoma is a benign lump of fat that can be removed if it is uncomfortable, growing, or unwanted. An epidermoid cyst, often called a sebaceous cyst, is best removed when it is not inflamed; an infected cyst is drained first and excised once it has settled, because removal of the whole wall is needed to prevent recurrence. Every excised specimen should be sent to the laboratory, and the patient should know how and when the result will be given.

## 15.4 Ingrowing toenails

Mild ingrowing toenails can be treated with warm soaks, lifting the nail edge, and cutting the nail straight across. When this fails, partial removal of the nail edge followed by phenol applied to the nail bed is more effective at preventing recurrence than surgical excision without phenol [2]. Check the circulation and sensation of the foot first: patients with diabetes or poor circulation need extra care and may need referral. Explain that the nail will be narrower afterward.

## 15.5 Skin abscesses

Incision and drainage is the main treatment for a skin abscess. Antibiotics are added for surrounding cellulitis, signs of systemic infection, reduced immunity, or abscesses in difficult sites; in a large trial, an antibiotic after drainage modestly improved cure rates, so local resistance patterns and patient factors guide the decision [3]. Recurrent abscesses raise the possibility of diabetes or other underlying conditions. Abscesses in the perianal and pilonidal areas are covered in Chapters 7 and 8.

## 15.7 Preparation

- **Blood thinners.** Most minor skin procedures can be done without stopping anticoagulants or antiplatelet medicines; follow the plan in Chapter 22.

<!-- -->

- **Fasting and driving.** No fasting is needed for local anesthesia. Patients can usually drive home after small procedures, but not after toenail surgery on the right foot or procedures that limit movement.

- **Ramadan.** Some fasting patients ask whether the injection affects their fast. Offer an appointment after iftar or outside Ramadan if they prefer, and refer religious questions to their adviser.

- **Wound site.** Agree the incision line with the patient before marking, especially on the face, neck, chest, and shoulders.

## 15.8 Aftercare and stitch removal

Most wounds can be kept dry for 24 to 48 hours and then washed normally. Stitch removal depends on the site (Table 15.2). Scars are red and may be raised for some months before fading (Figure 15.2). Silicone gel or sheeting is recommended to help prevent abnormal scarring in patients at risk [5]. Keloids, raised scars that grow beyond the wound, are more common on the chest, shoulders, and earlobes and in people with darker skin; ask about previous keloids before operating on these sites.

**Table 15.2.** *Typical stitch removal times by site.*

| **Site**                       | **Stitches usually removed after** | **Notes**                                                 |
|--------------------------------|------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| **Face**                       | 5 to 7 days                        | Fine stitches; protect from the sun                       |
| **Scalp and neck**             | 7 to 10 days                       |                                                           |
| **Chest, back, and shoulders** | 10 to 14 days                      | Highest risk of wide or keloid scars                      |
| **Arms and legs**              | 10 to 14 days                      | Longer over joints                                        |
| **Hands and feet**             | 10 to 14 days                      | Elevate to reduce swelling                                |
| **Toenail after phenol**       | No stitches                        | Daily soaks and dressings for 2 to 4 weeks while it heals |

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

**“Will it hurt?”**

The numbing injection stings for a few seconds. After that you should feel only pushing and pulling. Tell me if you feel pain, and I will give more.

**“Will it leave a scar?”**

Yes. Every cut leaves a scar, usually a thin line that fades over months. Some areas, such as the chest and shoulders, scar more.

**“Is it cancer?”**

Most lumps like this are harmless. We send every specimen to the laboratory to be sure, and I will tell you the result.

**“Will it come back?”**

A lipoma or cyst removed completely rarely comes back. A cyst that is removed while inflamed is more likely to recur.

**“Can I drive home?”**

Usually yes after small procedures. Not after an operation on your right foot or one that limits your movement.

**“When can I wash and do ablution?”**

Keep the wound dry for 1 to 2 days, then wash normally. Use a waterproof dressing for ablution in the first days.

**“When can I go back to work?”**

Usually the same or next day, unless the wound is on a part of the body you use heavily at work.

**“Will my toenail grow back normally?”**

The nail will be a little narrower, which is what stops it growing in again.

**“I have had a raised scar before. Will it happen again?”**

It might, especially on the chest, shoulders, or earlobes. Tell us before the procedure, so that we can plan the cut and start scar treatment early.
