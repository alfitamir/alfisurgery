# Chapter 25 — Children and Young People

Operating on a child means caring for a family, not only a patient. Parents carry their own fear, often sharper than the child’s, and children sense it. Consent is legally the parent’s or guardian’s, but a child who is old enough to understand deserves an honest, age-appropriate explanation and a real say in how the day goes. Pain relief, fasting, and anxiety all need an approach specific to children, not a smaller version of the adult plan. This chapter covers consent and assent, preparing a child for the day, and the medicine differences that matter most.

**STEP 1 Consent, assent, and explaining the plan**

## 25.1 Consent and assent

Consent for a child’s operation is given by a parent or legal guardian, following local law and hospital policy. Even though a young child cannot give legal consent, the child’s own agreement, or assent, should be sought wherever possible, at a level suited to their age and understanding [1]. A toddler can be told simply what will happen to their body; a seven-year-old can be shown pictures and asked questions; a teenager can take part in the discussion much as an adult would, and many legal systems give older adolescents an increasing role in their own healthcare decisions. When a child clearly objects to a non-urgent procedure, this should be explored and, where possible, addressed rather than overridden.

## 25.2 Preparing the child and the family

Unmanaged preoperative anxiety is common in children and is linked to more distress during anesthesia induction and to difficult behaviors afterward, such as sleep problems and new fears [2]. Preparation reduces this. Honest, simple explanations; a chance to handle equipment or play with a mask; a comfort object from home; and a parent present during induction where the hospital allows it, all help. A calm parent helps create a calm child, so address parental anxiety directly as well (Figure 25.1).

## 25.3 Fasting in children

Fasting rules for children differ from adults and should not simply be copied across. Current European pediatric anesthesia guidance allows clear fluids until 1 hour before anesthesia, breast milk until 3 hours before, and formula or a light meal until 6 hours before, for healthy children [3, 4]. Unnecessarily long fasting in children causes distress, dehydration, and low blood sugar, and is a common avoidable source of a bad day for the whole family. Confirm the exact local protocol, since practice varies.

## 25.4 Medicines that are different in children

<td><p><strong>Caution: codeine and tramadol are contraindicated in children</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><p>Codeine is contraindicated for pain or cough in children under 12, and carries a boxed warning against use after tonsillectomy or adenoidectomy in children of any age, because some children rapidly convert it to morphine and can stop breathing [5].</p></li>
<li><p>Tramadol is contraindicated for pain in children under 12, and for pain after tonsillectomy or adenoidectomy in children under 18 [5].</p></li>
<li><p>Neither should be given to a breastfeeding mother, because of the same risk to the infant [5].</p></li>
<li><p>Use weight-based paracetamol and, where suitable, ibuprofen instead, with a short course of a different opioid only when specifically prescribed for significant pain.</p></li>
</ul></td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>

Paracetamol (acetaminophen) doses in children are calculated by weight: typically 15 mg/kg every 4 to 6 hours, with a maximum of 4 doses (60 mg/kg) in 24 hours, not exceeding the adult maximum; always check the local formulary and the product information, and use an age- and weight-appropriate preparation. Ibuprofen, when suitable, is typically 5 to 10 mg/kg every 6 to 8 hours. Give exact doses on the discharge instructions, calculated for the individual child’s weight, not as a generic range.

**STEP 2 Preparation, the day of surgery, and recovery**

## 25.5 Before the operation

- **Weight.** Record the child’s current weight for accurate medicine dosing.

<!-- -->

- **Vaccinations and illness.** A child with a current cough, cold, or fever may need the operation postponed; follow the anesthesia team’s advice.

- **Comfort items.** Bring a favorite toy, blanket, or dummy (pacifier).

- **Preparation materials.** Many units offer picture books, videos, or a pre-visit to the ward; use them where available.

- **Parents.** Explain what to expect in the anesthesia room and recovery area, and prepare them for how a child may look and behave on waking (groggy, tearful, or clingy is normal).

## 25.6 After the operation

Children often wake from anesthesia confused, tearful, or agitated for a short time (emergence agitation); this usually settles within 20 to 30 minutes with a calm, familiar adult present. Encourage early return to normal activity, play, and school as the specific procedure chapter advises, scaled to the child’s age. Dehydration and reluctance to drink are common concerns; offer small, frequent sips of a preferred drink.

**STEP 3 Questions parents ask**

**“Can I stay with my child until they are asleep?”**

In most units, yes, where it is safe to do so. Ask your anesthesia team about their policy.

**“Will my child remember the pain?”**

Children generally do not remember pain from under general anesthesia. Good pain relief afterward is still important, and we will give you a clear plan.

**“Can I give my child the cough medicine with codeine that I have at home?”**

No. Codeine is not safe for children under 12, and must not be used after tonsil or adenoid surgery in anyone under 18 [5]. Use only the medicines on the plan we give you.

**“Why can’t my child eat until so close to the operation now, when the old advice was midnight?”**

Modern guidance allows most children clear fluids until 1 hour before anesthesia, which is kinder and safer than fasting overnight [3].

**“My child is very frightened. What can we do?”**

This is common and manageable. Bring a comfort toy, use simple honest words, and ask the team about preparation materials. We will support both of you.

**“How much pain medicine should I give at home?”**

Exactly the dose on your discharge sheet, calculated for your child’s weight. Do not use adult doses or leftover medicines from another illness.

**“My teenager wants to make their own decisions. Is that allowed?”**

We involve young people in decisions about their own body as much as possible, alongside their parent or guardian, following our local policy.
