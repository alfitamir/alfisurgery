# Chapter 05 — Appendicitis and Appendicectomy

Appendicitis is the most common reason for emergency abdominal surgery, and the consultation is very different from the planned clinic visit of Chapter 4. The patient is often young, in pain, frightened, fasting, and seen at night, frequently with family on the telephone rather than in the room. There is less time, but the duties are the same: to explain the diagnosis, to offer the reasonable options, including antibiotics for some patients, to disclose the material risks, and to confirm that the patient has understood. This chapter shows how to do that well under time pressure.

**STEP 1 Explaining the disease and the treatment**

## 5.1 Consent when time is short

Emergency consent is still informed consent. Three practical steps make it possible.

- **Treat pain first.** A patient in severe pain cannot weigh information. Giving analgesia, including opioids, does not increase the risk of a missed or wrong diagnosis in acute abdominal pain [1], and it often makes the conversation possible.

<!-- -->

- **Check capacity.** Pain, fever, sepsis, and sedating medicines can all affect understanding. If capacity is in doubt, wait until analgesia has worked, assess it, and document the assessment (Section 1.10).

- **Involve the right people.** Ask the patient whom they want involved. A relative can join by telephone or video, and so can a professional interpreter. Confirm the decision with the patient directly.

Women of childbearing age are offered a pregnancy test before imaging and surgery. Patients under the age of legal consent are managed as described in Chapter 25.

## 5.2 Choosing the treatment

The decision depends on whether the appendicitis is uncomplicated or complicated, and on the patient’s preferences (Figure 5.1). Current guidelines support laparoscopic appendicectomy as the standard treatment, and an antibiotics-first strategy as a safe alternative for selected adults with uncomplicated appendicitis confirmed on imaging, provided they are told about the risk of recurrence [2].

The antibiotics-first figures need to be stated plainly. In the largest trial, about 3 in 10 patients treated with antibiotics had an appendicectomy within 90 days, rising to about 4 in 10 when an appendicolith was present [3]. By 1 year, about 4 in 10 had had an appendicectomy, and by 3 to 4 years about 5 in 10 [4]. In a European trial, about 4 in 10 had had an appendicectomy by 5 years [5]. Patients treated with antibiotics missed fewer days of work at first, but a second illness and a second admission are a real burden for many.

Timing also matters to patients waiting overnight. For uncomplicated appendicitis, delaying surgery by up to 24 hours after admission does not appear to increase the risk of perforation or of complications [6]. Patients with signs of perforation, peritonitis, or sepsis need surgery without unnecessary delay.

When the appendix is found to be normal, the surgeon looks for another cause. Whether a normal-looking appendix is removed varies between surgeons, and patients should know before the operation that it may happen. In a large UK study, a normal appendix was removed in about 12 in 100 men and 28 in 100 women who had an appendicectomy [7]; careful clinical scoring and imaging reduce this.

## 5.4 When the appendix has burst or formed an abscess

Perforation changes the recovery and the risks. After surgery for complex appendicitis, 2 days of intravenous antibiotics was as effective as 5 days in a large trial, with fewer side effects, and about 1 in 10 patients developed an infection, usually an abscess, within 90 days [8]. Patients should be prepared for a longer stay, slower return of appetite, and the possibility of a drain or further procedure.

When an abscess or inflammatory mass is found, initial treatment is usually antibiotics, with drainage if needed. The appendix may then be removed weeks later (interval appendicectomy). One reason is the risk of an underlying tumor: in a large prospective study, a tumor was found in about 14 in 100 patients with an appendiceal abscess, compared with about 2 in 100 with uncomplicated appendicitis, and interval appendicectomy was recommended for patients older than 35 years [9, 10].

**STEP 2 Preparation, medicines, and recovery**

## 5.5 Before the operation

- **Fasting and fluids.** Explain when the patient can no longer eat or drink, and give intravenous fluids. Avoid unnecessarily long fasts; the anesthesia team decides on clear fluids if surgery is delayed.

- **Medicines.** Record all medicines, including blood thinners, diabetes medicines, and weight-loss injections, and tell the anesthesia team (Chapters 18, 22, and 23). An emergency does not leave time for the usual stopping intervals, so these are managed individually.

- **Antibiotics.** A single preoperative dose is standard. Explain that further antibiotics are needed only if the appendix has burst.

- **Fasting for Ramadan.** Patients who are fasting need to stop for treatment. Illness is a recognized reason not to fast; patients can be referred to their religious adviser if they wish.

## 5.6 After the operation and discharge

After uncomplicated appendicitis, most patients eat and walk the same day and go home the next day; some go home the same day. After perforation, appetite and bowel function return more slowly, and discharge follows when the patient is eating, the pain is controlled with tablets, and fever has settled. Complete the personal instruction card (Appendix A), confirm the warning signs with teach-back, and say how the pathology result will be given.

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

**“Can I just take antibiotics instead of having the operation?”**

If your scan shows a simple inflamed appendix, yes, that is a reasonable choice. About 7 in 10 people avoid an operation in the first 3 months, but about half need the operation within 3 to 4 years [3, 4]. If there is a small stone in the appendix, antibiotics fail more often, and we usually advise the operation.

**“Why can’t I have the operation right now? I have been waiting all night.”**

I understand it is hard to wait. For a simple appendicitis, a wait of up to a day does not make it more likely to burst or to cause complications [6]. If your condition changes, we will move faster.

**“Can I have something for the pain before you decide? Won’t it hide the problem?”**

Yes, you can, and you should. Pain relief does not stop us from making the right diagnosis [1].

**“Do I need my appendix?”**

No. You can live a normal, healthy life without it.

**“What if my appendix turns out to be normal?”**

That happens to some people, more often women, because other conditions can look the same [7]. We will look for another cause during the operation and tell you what we found.

**“Why do I need more antibiotics after the operation?”**

Only if the appendix had burst. Then a short course helps clear infection inside the tummy. Two days of drip antibiotics works as well as five for most people [8].

**“The appendix had burst. What does that mean for me?”**

You will stay longer, recover more slowly, and have a higher chance of a collection of infection, about 1 in 10 [8]. Most people still recover fully.

**“Why do I need another operation later?”**

When an abscess is treated with antibiotics, the appendix is still there. In people over about 35, a hidden tumor is found in a significant number, so we usually advise removing the appendix a few weeks later [9].

**“Can appendicitis come back after the appendix is removed?”**

No. Very rarely, a small stump of the appendix left behind becomes inflamed, but this is uncommon.

**“I am pregnant. Is it safe?”**

Appendicitis in pregnancy needs prompt treatment. Keyhole surgery can be done safely in any trimester by an experienced team [11]. Antibiotics alone have not been well studied in pregnancy.

**“Why is my appendix sent to the laboratory?”**

Every appendix is checked under a microscope. Almost all results show only inflammation. About 1 or 2 in 100 show a small tumor, most of which are cured by removing the appendix; a few need further treatment [9].

**“I was fasting for Ramadan. Can I continue?”**

You need to stop fasting for your treatment and recovery. Illness is a recognized reason to delay fasting. You can fast again when you are eating normally and no longer need regular medicines.

**What is the appendix?**

The appendix is a small tube, about the size of a finger. It is joined to the large bowel, low on the right side of your tummy. You can live a normal life without it.

**What is appendicitis?**

Appendicitis means the appendix is blocked and swollen. It causes tummy pain, often starting near the belly button and moving to the lower right side. If it is not treated, it can burst and spread infection inside the tummy.

**Your treatment choices**

- **Operation (the usual treatment).** The appendix is removed by keyhole surgery. This cures the problem.

- **Antibiotics only (for some people).** If your scan shows a simple appendicitis, antibiotics may be enough. Most people avoid an operation at first, but about half need it within a few years.

- Your surgeon will explain which choices suit you. Ask as many questions as you need.

**What happens in the operation?**

- You will be fully asleep.

- The surgeon makes 3 small cuts and uses a small camera to remove the appendix.

- It usually takes less than 1 hour. Most people go home the next day.

- If the appendix has burst, you will need antibiotics and may stay a few more days.

- Sometimes the appendix looks normal, or the surgeon finds another problem. We will tell you what we found.

**1. Pain and pain medicines**

- Your tummy will be sore for a few days. Some people have shoulder pain for 1 to 2 days, from the gas used in the operation.

- Take your pain medicines regularly at first, as we told you.

- Strong pain medicines can make you sleepy and constipated. Do not drive while you take them.

**2. Your wounds**

- Keep the dressings clean and dry for 2 days.

- Some bruising is normal.

- If you have stitches or clips to take out, the date is on your card.

**3. Eating, drinking, and your bowels**

- Start with light meals and eat normally when you feel ready.

- If your appendix had burst, your appetite may take a few days to come back.

- Drink water, eat fruit and vegetables, and walk to avoid constipation.

**4. Rest, washing, and clothes**

- You may feel tired for 1 to 2 weeks. Rest, but get up and walk often.

- You can shower after 2 days. Pat the wounds dry.

- Do not soak in a bath or swim until the wounds have healed.

- Wear loose clothes.

**5. Getting back to normal**

These are usual times. If your appendix had burst, it may take longer. Your own plan is on your card.
