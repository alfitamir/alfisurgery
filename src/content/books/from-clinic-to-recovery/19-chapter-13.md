# Chapter 13 — Obesity and Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery: Choosing Treatment and Living Well Afterward

Obesity is a chronic, relapsing disease, not a failure of willpower. Most people who come to a bariatric clinic have tried many diets, often for years, and many have already used weight-loss injections. They arrive with hope, with fear of surgery, and often with advice from friends who had operations, sometimes abroad. The surgeon’s role is to help each patient understand all the options, from lifestyle change and medicines to endoscopic procedures and surgery, to choose the one that suits their health and life, and to prepare them for the lifelong changes that follow. Good education before surgery, and good follow-up afterward, prevent many of the complications described in this chapter.

**STEP 1 Explaining obesity and the choices**

## 13.1 Talking about weight respectfully

Many patients have experienced judgment from health professionals, and this makes them less likely to seek help. Ask permission before discussing weight, use person-first language (“a person with obesity”), and avoid words such as “fat” or “morbidly obese.” Weigh patients in private, with equipment that fits. Explain obesity as a disease of appetite and metabolism that the body defends: when weight falls, hunger rises and energy use falls, which is why diets alone so often fail. Patients are relieved to hear that their struggle has a biological explanation.

## 13.2 The range of treatments

Every patient should understand the full range of options and what each can realistically achieve (Figure 13.1). A structured lifestyle program typically achieves about 9 in 100 of body weight at one year [1], and remains the foundation of every other treatment. Among medicines, weekly tirzepatide led to an average loss of about 20 in 100 of body weight at 72 weeks, and semaglutide about 14 in 100, in a head-to-head trial [2]. These medicines work while they are taken; after semaglutide was stopped, about two-thirds of the lost weight returned within a year [3]. Endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty, performed through the mouth, achieved about 14 in 100 at one year [4]. Surgery produces the largest and most durable loss: in a large cohort, average loss at 5 years was about 19 in 100 after sleeve gastrectomy and about 26 in 100 after gastric bypass [5].

## 13.3 Who is suitable for surgery

Current international criteria recommend metabolic and bariatric surgery for adults with a BMI of 35 or more, regardless of other conditions, and suggest considering it for those with a BMI of 30 to 34.9 who have metabolic disease, such as type 2 diabetes [6]. Lower thresholds apply to people of Asian descent, in whom metabolic disease occurs at a lower BMI [6]. Surgery is also an option for selected adolescents with severe obesity (Chapter 25).

Suitability depends on more than BMI. A multidisciplinary assessment, usually including a surgeon, dietitian, psychologist or psychiatrist, and physician, checks that the patient understands the lifelong changes, has stable mental health, has stopped smoking, and has a plan for contraception. Untreated eating disorders, active substance misuse, and inability to follow lifelong supplements and follow-up are reasons to delay surgery and offer support first.

## 13.4 Choosing the operation

Sleeve gastrectomy and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass are the two most common operations, and both give good, lasting results. In a randomized trial followed for 10 years, the loss of excess weight was 44 in 100 after sleeve and 51 in 100 after bypass, and reflux inflammation of the esophagus was seen in 31 in 100 after sleeve compared with 7 in 100 after bypass; Barrett esophagus was uncommon and similar after both, at about 4 in 100 [7]. In patients with type 2 diabetes, surgery achieved near-normal blood sugar without diabetes medicines far more often than intensive medical therapy at 5 years [8]. **STEP 2 Preparation, medicines, and lifelong follow-up**

## 13.5 Before the operation

- **Assessment.** Blood tests, including vitamins and iron; endoscopy where the unit’s protocol or reflux symptoms require it; review by the dietitian and psychologist.

<!-- -->

- **Liver-shrinking diet.** Many units ask patients to follow a low-calorie diet for about two weeks before surgery to make the operation safer.

- **Smoking.** Stop completely. Smoking increases ulcers after bypass and other complications.

- **Contraception.** Women should avoid pregnancy until weight has stabilized, usually 12 to 18 months after surgery [9]. After bypass, absorption of oral contraceptive pills may be unreliable, so other methods are often advised.

- **Medicines.** Weight-loss injections, diabetes medicines, and blood thinners need individual plans (Chapters 18, 22, and 23).

## 13.6 Eating after surgery

After surgery, food is reintroduced in stages over about six weeks (Figure 13.3). The rules are simple, and patients should hear them several times: eat protein first, aiming for at least 60 g a day; take small bites and chew well; stop at the first sign of fullness; sip water between meals rather than with them; and avoid sugary drinks and grazing [9]. Hair thinning in the first months is common and usually recovers.

## 13.7 Lifelong supplements and follow-up

All patients need lifelong supplements, usually a complete multivitamin with minerals, calcium with vitamin D, vitamin B12, and iron where needed, with higher needs after bypass, and regular blood tests to detect deficiency [9]. Explain why this matters: deficiencies develop silently and can cause anemia, bone thinning, and nerve damage. Persistent vomiting can cause severe thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency within weeks, which is an emergency. Follow-up with the bariatric team is lifelong, and patients who had surgery elsewhere, including abroad, should be welcomed into local follow-up.

## 13.8 Ramadan, pregnancy, and daily life

Fasting during the first months after surgery risks dehydration and inadequate protein. Most teams advise against fasting during the first 6 to 12 months; after that, many patients can fast with a plan for protein and fluids between iftar and suhoor and supplements taken at night, agreed with the dietitian. Pregnancy after surgery is usually safe once weight has stabilized, but needs early notification of the team, nutritional monitoring, and an alternative to the standard glucose tolerance test, which can cause dumping after bypass.

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

**“Isn’t surgery the easy way out?”**

No. Obesity is a medical condition, and surgery is a medical treatment for it, like any other. It takes real commitment before and after, for life.

**“Why not just take the injections?”**

They are a good option for many people, and work well while you take them. Most people need them long-term, because weight usually returns when they stop [3]. Surgery gives more lasting weight loss for most people with severe obesity [5].

**“Will I regain weight?”**

Some regain after the first few years is common and normal. Large regain is less common, and can be treated with diet support, medicines, or sometimes further surgery.

**“Sleeve or bypass: which is better for me?”**

Both work well. If you have bad heartburn or long-standing diabetes, a bypass is often better. If you take anti-inflammatory medicines regularly or want a simpler operation with fewer vitamin problems, a sleeve may suit you better. We will decide together (Figure 13.2).

**“Will my diabetes go away?”**

Many people come off some or all of their diabetes medicines. Surgery achieved near-normal blood sugar without medicines far more often than medical treatment alone [8]. Diabetes can return in later years, so checks continue.

**“Is the operation safe?”**

It is one of the safer major operations. The risk of dying is about 5 in 10,000 after a sleeve and about 9 in 10,000 after a bypass [10], and the health benefits of weight loss are large.

**“Will I need vitamins forever?”**

Yes. Your body absorbs less from food after surgery. Without supplements, deficiencies develop slowly and can cause serious problems [9].

**“Can I get pregnant afterward?”**

Yes, and fertility often improves. Wait 12 to 18 months until your weight is stable, use reliable contraception until then, and tell us as soon as you are pregnant [9].

**“Will I have loose skin?”**

Often, after large weight loss. Some people choose body-contouring surgery once weight has been stable for some time.

**“Can I have the operation abroad?”**

Many people do, but the operation is only the beginning. Make sure you have lifelong follow-up and blood tests arranged, and bring your operation records to us.

**“Can I fast in Ramadan?”**

Usually not in the first 6 to 12 months. After that, many people can fast with a plan for protein, fluids, and vitamins, agreed with the dietitian.

**“Will my mood change?”**

Many people feel better, but some find the changes difficult. A small increase in alcohol problems and in self-harm has been reported after surgery [11]. We will ask about your mood at every visit, and you can always talk to us.

**Your new life: the five golden rules**

- **Protein first** at every meal. Aim for at least 60 g of protein a day.
