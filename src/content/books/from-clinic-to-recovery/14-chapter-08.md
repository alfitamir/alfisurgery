# Chapter 08 — Pilonidal Disease

Pilonidal disease mainly affects young adults, often students, soldiers, drivers, and office workers who sit for long periods, and it is particularly common in hot climates. For these patients the important questions are practical: how long they will be off school or work, how long a wound will take to heal, whether they will be able to sit, pray, and drive, and whether the disease will come back. Treatment choices range from small procedures that allow return to normal life within days to larger operations with longer recovery, and recurrence depends strongly on the technique. Good counseling helps each patient choose the option that fits both the disease and their life.

**STEP 1 Explaining the disease and the treatment**

## 8.1 Three forms of the disease

Guidelines distinguish three presentations [1, 2]. Pits found by chance without symptoms do not need treatment. An acute abscess needs prompt drainage, and the incision is best placed to one side of the midline [3]; many patients need no further surgery, and a definitive procedure is offered if symptoms return. Chronic disease, with recurring discharge or pain, is treated with a definitive procedure chosen according to its extent: minimally invasive techniques for simple disease, and excision for extensive or recurrent disease [2]. ## 8.3 Choosing the operation

Recurrence after pilonidal surgery depends strongly on the technique and on how long patients are followed. In a large meta-analysis, excision with closure in the midline had a recurrence of about 17 in 100 at 5 years, compared with about 2 in 100 after the Karydakis or Bascom cleft-lift operations, which close the wound to one side of the midline; recurrence after midline closure continued to rise over 20 years [4]. Midline closure should therefore be avoided [1]. Minimally invasive procedures cause less pain and allow a quicker return to work, and are suitable for simple disease, accepting a higher chance of recurrence [1, 2]. Table 8.2 compares the options for patients.

**STEP 2 Preparation, medicines, and recovery**

## 8.4 Before the operation

- **Timing.** Ask about examinations, military service, travel, and work. Many patients choose a minimally invasive procedure or a date that allows the recovery they need.

<!-- -->

- **Risk factors.** Stopping smoking and losing excess weight reduce wound problems; these can be addressed before a planned procedure [2].

- **Infection.** Antibiotics are not a substitute for drainage of an abscess. An acute abscess is drained first, and definitive surgery is planned once the inflammation has settled.

- **Medicines and fasting.** Use the standard plans in Chapters 2, 18, 22, and 23. Most procedures are day cases.

## 8.5 Wound care after the operation

Wound care differs with the type of operation, and patients need clear, written instructions for their own wound. After minimally invasive procedures, the small wounds are washed in the shower and covered with a light dressing. After off-midline closure, the wound is kept dry for the first days, a drain may be removed in clinic, and prolonged sitting directly on the wound is avoided until it is comfortable. An open wound is showered daily and dressed or packed by the patient, a family member, or a nurse, often for several weeks; patients should know who will help and how to obtain dressings. Keeping hair away from a healing wound is often advised.

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

**“Why did I get this?”**

Loose hairs collect in the groove between the buttocks and are pushed into the skin, especially with sitting, sweating, and friction. It is common in young adults and is not caused by poor hygiene.

**“Is it contagious?”**

No. You cannot catch it from someone or give it to anyone.

**“The pits do not bother me. Do I need an operation?”**

No. Pits without symptoms do not need treatment [1]. Come back if you notice pain, swelling, or discharge.

**“Can antibiotics cure it?”**

Antibiotics can calm surrounding infection, but they cannot remove the hair and tunnels. An abscess needs to be drained.

**“Will it come back?”**

It can. The chance depends on the operation: low after a flap that moves the scar away from the middle, about 2 in 100 at 5 years; higher after smaller procedures; and highest after a midline closure, which we avoid [4].

**“Why leave the wound open?”**

An open wound heals from the bottom up and avoids tension on stitches. It takes weeks of dressings, so we discuss whether it suits your life before choosing it.

**“Do I need to shave or have laser hair removal for life?”**

Experts disagree. Hair removal may help some people, but it is not proven to stop the disease coming back [2]. Keeping the area clean and dry is helpful for everyone.

**“When can I sit, study, or go back to work?”**

Within days after a small procedure, about 2 weeks after a flap, and when sitting is comfortable with an open wound. We will plan around your examinations or work.

**“Can I pray normally?”**

You can pray from the first day. If sitting on the floor stretches or hurts the wound, pray seated on a chair until it is comfortable.

**“Can it become cancer?”**

This is extremely rare and happens only in disease left untreated for many years.

**What is pilonidal disease?**

Loose hairs collect in the groove between the buttocks. Sitting and sweating push them into the skin. They make small holes, called pits, and a tunnel under the skin that can get infected. It is common in young adults. It is not caused by being unclean, and you cannot pass it to anyone.

**The treatment you had**

☐ Drainage of an abscess

☐ Small procedure to clean out the pits (pit picking, endoscopic, or laser)

☐ Operation with the wound closed to one side (flap)

☐ Operation with the wound left open to heal

**1. Pain and pain medicines**

- The area will be sore, especially when sitting. This gets better each week.

- Take your pain medicines regularly at first, as we told you.

- Use a soft cushion when sitting.

**2. Wound care**

- **Small procedure or drainage:** shower daily and cover with a light dressing.

- **Closed wound (flap):** keep it dry for the first days, as we told you. If you have a drain, we will remove it in clinic. Avoid sitting directly on the wound for long.

- **Open wound:** shower once or twice a day and let the water run over the wound. Then pack or dress it as we showed you.

- Keep hair away from the wound while it heals.

**3. Eating and drinking**

- Eat and drink normally. Protein, fruit, and vegetables help healing.

- Avoid constipation, because straining is uncomfortable.

**4. Rest, washing, and clothes**

- Walk around from the first day.

- Wear loose cotton underwear and clothes that do not rub.

- Do not sit for long periods. Stand up and walk every half hour.
