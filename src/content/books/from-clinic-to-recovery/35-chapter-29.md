# Chapter 29 — Driving, Flying, Exercise, and Return to Work: Evidence-Based Readiness Criteria

Patients and employers constantly ask for a single number: when can I drive, fly, exercise, or go back to work? Procedure chapters give typical ranges for their own operation. This chapter gathers the general, evidence-based criteria that apply across operations, so that a specific date can be reasoned from a patient’s actual recovery rather than copied from a leaflet. These are readiness criteria, not countdown timers: two patients having the same operation can be ready on different days.

## 29.1 Driving

No single law covers every situation, and advice differs by country, license type, and insurer, so readers should also confirm local licensing-authority guidance. The functional test used throughout this book is a reasonable general standard: a patient can safely return to driving when they can perform an emergency stop without hesitation or significant pain, can turn fully to check mirrors and blind spots, are no longer taking opioids or other medicines that cause drowsiness, and feel confident in an emergency. For major abdominal or pelvic surgery, many units advise against driving for about 2 weeks at a minimum, and some patients need longer; after day-case or minor procedures, 24 to 48 hours is often enough once the above criteria are met. Patients should also check with their motor insurer, since some policies specify a minimum interval after surgery or anesthesia.

**Table 29.1.** *Functional readiness criteria for driving after surgery.*

| **Readiness criterion**          | **What to check**                                                                     |
|----------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Emergency stop**               | Can press the brake fully and quickly, without wincing or hesitation                  |
| **Mirror and blind-spot checks** | Can turn the head and torso fully without pain                                        |
| **Medicines**                    | Not taking opioids, sedating antihistamines, or other medicines that impair alertness |
| **Confidence and concentration** | Feels alert and able to react; not distracted by pain                                 |
| **Insurance**                    | Checked with the insurer for any procedure-specific exclusion                         |

## 29.2 Flying

The main aviation concern after abdominal surgery is that gas trapped in the bowel or abdomen expands at cabin altitude, which can cause pain or, rarely, threaten a fresh repair. UK Civil Aviation Authority guidance for health professionals advises avoiding air travel for about 10 days after open abdominal surgery and for about 24 hours after laparoscopic surgery, because of residual gas in each case [1]. Longer intervals of several weeks to a few months are advised after major open abdominal or pelvic operations such as hysterectomy or colectomy, allowing for full recovery and a satisfactory follow-up review; this is a general aviation guideline and individual surgical advice may differ. For any patient with a chest drain, recent pneumothorax, or significant anemia, seek specific advice before booking a flight. General anesthesia itself is not a reason to delay flying once the surgical criteria above are met.

**Table 29.2.** *General flying guidance after surgery, based on UK Civil Aviation Authority advice for health professionals. Always confirm with the treating surgeon.*

| **After**                                                            | **General guideline**                  | **Notes**                                                                |
|----------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Laparoscopic surgery**                                             | About 24 hours                         | Because of residual gas in the abdomen [1]                             |
| **Open abdominal surgery**                                           | About 10 days minimum                  | Risk of gas expansion affecting a fresh wound or repair [1]            |
| **Major abdominal or pelvic surgery (e.g. hysterectomy, colectomy)** | Several weeks, sometimes 2 to 3 months | Depends on full recovery and follow-up; shorter after smaller operations |
| **Minor procedures, no significant gas or bleeding risk**            | Often within days                      | Confirm with the surgical team                                           |

## 29.3 Exercise, swimming, and heavy lifting

Light walking should start on the day of surgery or the next day for almost every operation, because early mobilization protects against clots and chest complications (Chapter 2). Return to structured exercise and heavy lifting is guided by wound comfort and type of surgery rather than a single rule: superficial and minor procedures often allow normal exercise within 1 to 2 weeks; most abdominal and pelvic operations need about 4 to 6 weeks before heavy lifting or contact sport; and operations involving a hernia repair or mesh, or major reconstruction, may need individual guidance from the surgical team. Swimming is generally deferred until the wound is fully healed and watertight, usually 1 to 2 weeks for small wounds and longer for larger ones.

## 29.4 Return to work

Return to work depends on the physical demands of the job as much as the operation itself. A desk-based worker often returns sooner than someone whose job involves heavy lifting, prolonged standing, or driving for a living. Discuss the specific duties of the patient’s job, and consider a phased return (reduced hours or lighter duties at first) for physically demanding roles. Procedure chapters give typical ranges for office work and manual work separately; use these as a starting point and adjust for the individual’s actual job and recovery.

## 29.5 Questions patients ask

**“Can I drive the day after my operation?”**

Only if you meet the readiness criteria: a pain-free emergency stop, full mirror checks, no sedating medicines, and feeling confident. For many operations this takes longer than a day; check your specific procedure chapter.

**“I have a long-haul flight booked. Is it safe?”**

It depends on your operation and how soon after surgery the flight is. Tell your surgical team your travel dates as early as possible so we can advise, and so you can rearrange the booking if needed.

**“My job involves heavy lifting. When can I go back?”**

Tell us exactly what your job involves. We can often suggest a phased return with lighter duties at first, rather than an all-or-nothing date.

**“Can I go to the gym two weeks after my operation?”**

Light exercise is often fine by then, but heavy lifting and contact sport usually need longer. Let comfort and your procedure chapter guide you.
