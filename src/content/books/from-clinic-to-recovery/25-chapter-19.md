# Chapter 19 — Lung Disease, Sleep Apnea, and Surgery

Lung complications, such as pneumonia, collapse of parts of the lung, and breathing failure, are among the most common serious problems after surgery, and they lengthen hospital stays more than many surgical complications. Patients with asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), or obstructive sleep apnea, and those who smoke, are at higher risk. Much of this risk can be reduced by preparation before surgery and by simple actions afterward that patients and families can learn. In the Gulf, the rising prevalence of obesity means that undiagnosed sleep apnea is common among surgical patients, and the widespread use of shisha and vaping adds to the risk.

**STEP 1 Explaining the risk and making the plan**

## 19.1 Who is at risk

The ARISCAT score identifies the main predictors of postoperative pulmonary complications: older age, low preoperative oxygen saturation, a respiratory infection in the previous month, anemia, an upper abdominal or chest incision, an operation lasting more than 2 hours, and emergency surgery [1]. Smoking, COPD, asthma, and sleep apnea add to the risk. Figure 19.1 links each risk factor to an action that patients and teams can take.

## 19.2 Asthma

Patients with well-controlled asthma rarely have serious problems. Ask about recent attacks, hospital admissions, and courses of oral steroids. Patients should continue their usual inhalers, including on the morning of surgery, and bring their reliever inhaler to hospital [2]. Elective surgery is best delayed if asthma is poorly controlled or after a recent attack. Patients who have taken oral steroids recently may need steroid cover (Chapter 21). Some patients with asthma react to anti-inflammatory medicines such as ibuprofen; ask before prescribing them for pain.

## 19.3 COPD

For patients with COPD, check symptoms, recent exacerbations, oxygen saturation, and inhaler technique. Continue regular inhalers, review whether pulmonary rehabilitation is possible before major elective surgery, and delay elective surgery until an exacerbation has fully resolved [3]. Patients on home oxygen need a plan for oxygen in hospital and at discharge. Discuss the higher risk of chest infection, prolonged oxygen need, and possible intensive care admission honestly, in natural frequencies where unit data are available. Preoperative inspiratory muscle training reduced lung complications in trials of major surgery and may be offered where available [4].

## 19.4 Obstructive sleep apnea

Obstructive sleep apnea is common in surgical patients, especially those with obesity, and is often undiagnosed. It increases the risk of airway difficulty during anesthesia and of breathing problems after surgery, particularly with opioids and sedatives. Screen all adults with the STOP-Bang questionnaire (Table 19.2); a score of 5 or more, or 3 or more with certain features, indicates a high likelihood of moderate to severe sleep apnea [5]. Patients with known sleep apnea should bring their CPAP machine and use it in hospital. Patients with suspected but undiagnosed sleep apnea can usually proceed with surgery, with precautions, unless they have uncontrolled related conditions such as heart failure or very low oxygen levels [6].

**Table 19.2.** _The STOP-Bang screening questionnaire for obstructive sleep apnea._

| **Letter** | **Question**                                               | **Score 1 point if yes** |
| ---------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| **S**      | Snoring: do you snore loudly?                              | Yes                      |
| **T**      | Tired: do you often feel tired or sleepy during the day?   | Yes                      |
| **O**      | Observed: has anyone seen you stop breathing during sleep? | Yes                      |
| **P**      | Pressure: do you have high blood pressure?                 | Yes                      |
| **B**      | BMI above 35                                               | Yes                      |

## 19.5 Smoking, shisha, and vaping

Smoking increases wound and lung complications, and stopping before surgery reduces them; at least 4 weeks is ideal, but stopping at any time is worthwhile (Chapter 2) [7, 8]. Ask specifically about shisha (waterpipe) and vaping, which many patients do not regard as smoking. Offer support and nicotine replacement (Chapter 27).

**STEP 2 Preparation and recovery**

## 19.6 Before the operation

- **Stop smoking**, including shisha and vaping, as early as possible.

<!-- -->

- **Inhalers.** Continue them, check technique, and bring them to hospital.

- **CPAP.** Bring the machine, mask, and tubing, labeled with the patient’s name.

- **Chest infection.** Treat it and delay elective surgery until recovered.

- **Activity.** Walk daily; offer inspiratory muscle training or pulmonary rehabilitation before major surgery where available.

## 19.7 After the operation: I COUGH

A simple bundle called I COUGH (incentive breathing, coughing, oral care, understanding, getting out of bed, and head of bed elevation) reduced postoperative pneumonia when introduced as a hospital-wide program [9] (Figure 19.2). Incentive spirometry used alone has not been shown to prevent lung complications after abdominal surgery [10], so it should be part of the bundle, not a replacement for walking and good pain control.

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

**“Should I take my inhalers on the morning of the operation?”**

Yes. Take your usual inhalers and bring your reliever with you.

**“Why do you ask about my snoring?”**

Loud snoring and stopping breathing during sleep can be signs of sleep apnea, which affects how we give anesthesia and pain medicines [5].

**“Do I need to bring my CPAP machine?”**

Yes, please. You will use it after the operation and every night in hospital.

**“I only smoke shisha. Does that count?”**

Yes. Shisha and vaping also affect your lungs and healing. Stopping before surgery helps.

**“Why do you want me out of bed so soon?”**

Sitting up and walking open your lungs and prevent chest infections and clots. It is part of your treatment.

**“It hurts to cough. Should I avoid it?”**

No. Hold a pillow over your wound and cough gently. Tell us if pain stops you from coughing or breathing deeply, and we will adjust your pain relief.

**“I had a chest infection last week. Can I still have my operation?”**

For a planned operation, it is usually safer to wait until you have fully recovered.

**Before your operation**

- **Stop smoking now**, including shisha and vaping. Ask us for help.

- Keep taking your inhalers, including on the morning of the operation. Bring them with you.

- **If you have sleep apnea, bring your CPAP machine**, mask, and tubing.

- If you get a chest infection before your operation, tell us. We may need to wait.

- Walk every day to keep your lungs strong.

**After your operation: I COUGH**

_[Figure — diagram omitted in this text edition; see the figure caption below and the Word chapter file for the image]_

- Take 10 slow, deep breaths every hour while you are awake.

- Cough while holding a pillow over your wound.

- Brush your teeth and rinse your mouth twice a day.

- Sit up in a chair for meals and walk several times a day.

- Rest with your head raised.

- Use your CPAP every time you sleep, including naps.

- Tell us if pain stops you from breathing deeply or coughing.

**At home**

- Keep walking every day and doing your deep breaths.

- Keep using your inhalers and CPAP.

- Stay smoke-free. Your lungs keep healing.
