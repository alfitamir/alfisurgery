# Chapter 23 — GLP-1 and GIP/GLP-1 Receptor Agonists: Weight-Loss and Diabetes Injections

Weekly injections such as semaglutide and tirzepatide, and related daily injections and tablets, have become some of the most widely used medicines in the world, for diabetes and for weight loss. Many surgical patients take them, sometimes bought online or abroad, and do not always mention them because they do not think of them as “medicines.” These drugs slow stomach emptying, so food can remain in the stomach despite normal fasting, raising concern about aspiration during anesthesia. Guidance has changed quickly and still differs between societies. This chapter explains how to identify these patients, how to apply current guidance, and how to counsel them about other surgical effects of the medicines.

**STEP 1 Understanding the medicines and the guidance**

## 23.1 Why these medicines matter for surgery

GLP-1 receptor agonists and dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists reduce appetite and slow stomach emptying. This effect is strongest when the medicine is started or the dose is increased, and in patients who have nausea, vomiting, bloating, or early fullness. Retained food in the stomach at the start of anesthesia increases the risk of aspiration into the lungs. The medicines also affect other aspects of surgical care: they are associated with gallbladder and biliary disease [1], they can cause dehydration through nausea and reduced intake, and they increase the risk of low glucose when combined with insulin or sulfonylureas.

## 23.2 What current guidance says

Three major guidance documents agree that most patients can continue these medicines before elective surgery, but differ in how they manage the risk of retained stomach contents (Table 23.2). Each hospital should adopt one approach, agreed between anesthesia, surgery, and endocrinology, and apply it consistently.

**Table 23.2.** *Current guidance on GLP-1 and GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists before elective surgery.*

| **Guidance**                                            | **Main recommendations**                                                                                                                                                                                            |
|---------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Multisociety clinical practice guidance, 2024 [2]** | Most patients continue the medicine. Patients at higher risk (dose escalation, higher doses, gastrointestinal symptoms) may follow a liquid diet for 24 hours before surgery, or have the anesthetic plan adjusted. |
| **UK multidisciplinary consensus, 2025 [3]**          | Continue the medicine, with individual risk assessment and an anesthetic plan for patients at higher risk.                                                                                                          |
| **SPAQI multidisciplinary consensus, 2025 [4]**       | Continue the medicine, but extend the preoperative fast, including 24 hours without solid food for patients without significant symptoms.                                                                           |

## 23.4 Other surgical issues

- **Gallstones.** These medicines are associated with an increased risk of gallbladder and biliary disease [1]; consider gallstones in a patient with upper abdominal pain (Chapter 4).

<!-- -->

- **Nutrition and muscle.** Rapid weight loss can reduce muscle and protein stores. Before major surgery, check intake and encourage protein.

- **Low glucose.** In patients also taking insulin or sulfonylureas, reduced intake around surgery increases the risk of hypoglycemia (Chapter 18).

- **Contraception.** Tirzepatide can reduce the effectiveness of oral contraceptive pills; women should use an additional method for 4 weeks after starting and after each dose increase, according to the product information.

- **Pregnancy.** These medicines are stopped before a planned pregnancy, according to the product information and the prescriber’s advice.

- **After bariatric surgery.** They may be used as part of a team plan for weight regain (Chapter 13).

**STEP 2 Preparation, recovery, and Ramadan**

## 23.5 Before and after surgery

- **Plan.** Record the medicine details and the anesthetic plan in the notes, and tell the anesthesia team in advance.

- **Diet before surgery.** Follow the hospital protocol: for many patients, a clear liquid diet for 24 hours before surgery; standard fasting rules still apply on the day (Chapter 2).

- **Day of surgery.** Report any nausea, vomiting, or bloating on arrival.

- **After surgery.** Nausea may be more common. Restart the medicine when eating and drinking normally. If several doses have been missed, the prescriber may restart at a lower dose to avoid nausea.

## 23.6 Ramadan

Many people take these medicines while fasting, but nausea and reduced fluid intake increase the risk of dehydration. Patients should not fast while recovering from surgery, and those who wish to fast afterward should plan with their prescriber [5].

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

**“Do I need to stop my weight-loss injection before surgery?”**

Usually not. Most current guidance advises continuing it, with a liquid diet the day before for some people [2, 3]. Follow the plan we give you.

**“Why does it matter for the anesthetic?”**

These medicines slow how fast your stomach empties. Food can stay in your stomach even after normal fasting, which can be dangerous under anesthesia.

**“Why a liquid diet the day before?”**

Liquids leave the stomach faster than solid food, so your stomach is more likely to be empty for the operation.

**“I bought my injection online. Does that matter?”**

Please tell us. We need to know exactly what you are taking and when.

**“Can the injection cause gallstones?”**

It can increase the chance of gallbladder problems [1]. Tell us if you have pain in the upper right side of your tummy.

**“When can I restart it after the operation?”**

When you are eating and drinking normally. If you missed several doses, your doctor may restart a lower dose.

**“I take tirzepatide and the pill. Is that a problem?”**

Tirzepatide can make the pill less reliable. Use an extra method, such as condoms, for 4 weeks after starting and after each dose increase.

**Before your operation**

- **Do not stop or change your medicine on your own.**

- Tell us if you bought it online or abroad.

- If you are on a liquid diet the day before, take only clear liquids such as water, clear juice without pulp, clear soup, and jelly, as told.

- Tell us on arrival if you feel sick, have vomited, or feel bloated.

**After your operation**

- You may feel sick for a day or two. Eat small meals and drink often.

- Restart your medicine when you are eating and drinking normally.

- **If you take tirzepatide and the contraceptive pill:** use an extra method for 4 weeks after starting and after each dose increase.
