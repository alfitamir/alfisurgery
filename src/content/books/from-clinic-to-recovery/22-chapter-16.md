# Chapter 16 — Varicose Veins

Varicose veins are common, especially in people who stand for long periods at work, and in women after pregnancy. Some patients are troubled by aching, heaviness, and swelling; others by appearance; a smaller number develop skin damage, ulcers, or bleeding. Treatment has changed from open stripping under general anesthesia to walk-in, walk-out procedures under local anesthesia, and most patients can return to normal activity within days. The consultation needs to separate symptoms from cosmetic concerns, explain what treatment can and cannot achieve, and be honest that new varicose veins may appear over time.

**STEP 1 Explaining the condition and the treatment**

## 16.1 Who benefits from treatment

Varicose veins are caused by faulty valves in the superficial veins of the leg, which allow blood to flow backward and pool. Treatment is recommended for people with symptoms that affect their quality of life, swelling, skin changes, superficial thrombophlebitis, healed or active venous ulcers, or bleeding [1, 2]. Spider and thread veins are cosmetic and carry no health risk. Varicose veins without symptoms or skin changes rarely cause harm, and many patients are reassured to learn this. Every patient considered for treatment needs a duplex ultrasound scan to map which veins are faulty and to check the deep veins [1]. Treatment is planned on the scan, not on the visible veins alone.

## 16.2 Treatment options

Endothermal ablation, using radiofrequency or laser energy delivered through a catheter under local anesthesia, is recommended as the first treatment for incompetent truncal veins; foam sclerotherapy and surgery are alternatives when ablation is unsuitable [1, 2]. Non-thermal methods, such as cyanoacrylate glue, avoid the need for tumescent anesthesia and are increasingly used. In a randomized trial followed for 5 years, quality of life was better after laser ablation or surgery than after foam sclerotherapy, and laser ablation was the most cost-effective option [3]. Visible tributary veins can be removed at the same time through tiny incisions (phlebectomy) or treated with foam. Table 16.2 compares the options for patients.

**Table 16.2.** *Treatments for varicose veins, for use with patients.*

| **Treatment**                                      | **What happens**                                                          | **Recovery**                            | **Points to discuss**                                       |

## 16.3 Venous ulcers

Compression is the foundation of venous ulcer treatment. In addition, early endovenous ablation of the superficial veins speeds ulcer healing and reduces the time the ulcer remains open, compared with delaying treatment until the ulcer has healed [4]. Patients with an ulcer should be referred promptly for duplex assessment, and those with poor arterial circulation need assessment before compression is used.

**STEP 2 Preparation, medicines, and recovery**

## 16.4 Before the procedure

- **Pregnancy.** Treatment is usually deferred until after pregnancy, and ideally until a woman has completed her family, because pregnancy can cause new veins. Veins often improve after delivery.

<!-- -->

- **Clot risk.** Ask about previous clots, family history, and hormonal medicines. Some patients need a short course of blood-thinning injections after treatment, according to the unit protocol.

- **Blood thinners.** Most endovenous treatments can be done without stopping anticoagulants; follow Chapter 22.

- **Marking.** Veins to be treated are marked with the patient standing.

- **Practical points.** Bring loose trousers or a skirt to wear over the bandage and stocking; in hot weather, plan for the stocking.

## 16.5 After the procedure

Patients walk for about 30 minutes immediately after treatment and every day afterward. A tight, tender cord along the treated vein, with bruising, is common between about the third and the tenth day and is part of the vein closing. Compression stockings are often worn for a short period according to local practice. Long flights increase the risk of clots after treatment and are best avoided for some weeks; if unavoidable, the patient should discuss clot prevention with the team (Figure 16.2).

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

**“Are my varicose veins dangerous?”**

Usually not. They can cause aching, swelling, and skin problems, and occasionally bleed. They are not a sign of a clot in the deep veins.

**“Do I have to have them treated?”**

No, unless they cause symptoms, skin changes, an ulcer, or bleeding. If they do not bother you, leaving them is safe.

**“Will they come back?”**

The treated veins usually stay closed, but new varicose veins can form over the years. Some people need further treatment later.

**“Is laser better than surgery or foam?”**

Laser or radiofrequency ablation is usually the first choice, with a quick recovery. In a large trial, quality of life was better after laser or surgery than after foam [3]. The best option depends on your scan.

**“Will stockings cure my veins?”**

No. They relieve symptoms while you wear them, and are useful if you cannot or do not want treatment.

**“Can I have treatment before pregnancy?”**

It is usually better to wait until after you have completed your family, because pregnancy can cause new veins. Veins often improve after delivery.

**“I stand all day at work. What can I do?”**

Walk or move your calves often, raise your legs when resting, and wear stockings if they help. Treatment can help if you have symptoms.

**“Stockings are hard to wear in the heat. Do I have to?”**

After treatment, we may ask you to wear them for a short time. Lighter-weight stockings, putting them on early in the morning, and keeping skin dry help in hot weather.

**“What do I do if a vein bleeds?”**

Lie down, raise the leg above your heart, and press firmly on the spot with a clean cloth for at least 10 minutes. If it keeps bleeding, call an ambulance. Tell us afterward, because a vein that has bled should be treated.
