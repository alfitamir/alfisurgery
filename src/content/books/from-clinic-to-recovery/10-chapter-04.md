# Chapter 04 — Gallbladder Removal: Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy

> **Part II. Procedure-Specific Counseling, Consent, and Patient Guides** — Chapters 4 to 17

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is one of the most common operations in general surgery, and for many patients it is their first experience of an operation. It is usually a short day-case procedure with a quick recovery, yet the consultation is rarely simple. Not every abdominal pain in a patient with gallstones comes from the stones, patients worry about digestion without a gallbladder, families ask whether the stones can be dissolved instead, and the most serious complication, injury to the bile duct, is rare but must be discussed honestly. This chapter applies the five-step method of Part I to the operation.

**STEP 1 Explaining the disease and the operation**

## 4.1 Who is likely to benefit

The decision to operate starts with the symptoms, not the scan. Gallstones are common, and most never cause trouble: people whose stones are found by chance develop symptoms at a rate of only about 1 to 4 in 100 each year, and routine removal is not recommended for them [1]. Once typical attacks have started, about half of patients have further attacks, and complications develop in about 1 to 3 in 100 each year [1].

The type of pain matters. Typical biliary pain comes in severe attacks, is felt in the upper abdomen or under the right ribs, lasts at least 15 to 30 minutes, often spreads to the back, and responds to simple painkillers. In a large trial of patients with gallstones and abdominal pain, only about 60 in 100 were pain-free a year after usual care, which included surgery for most; vague symptoms such as bloating and indigestion were the ones least likely to improve [2]. Complications change the timing. For acute cholecystitis, early laparoscopic cholecystectomy, preferably within 7 days of admission and within 10 days of the onset of symptoms, is recommended over delayed surgery [3]. After mild gallstone pancreatitis, removing the gallbladder during the same admission reduces readmission for further gallstone complications [4]. Stones in the bile duct are cleared by endoscopy (ERCP) before, or by the surgeon during, the operation.

The last paragraph matters for consent. Safe cholecystectomy depends on positively identifying the structures before anything is divided (the critical view of safety), on imaging the bile ducts when the anatomy is uncertain, and on using a “bail-out” strategy, such as subtotal cholecystectomy or conversion, when the critical view cannot be achieved [5]. Patients who have heard about these possibilities in clinic are not alarmed if they happen.

## 4.3 Alternatives, including waiting

Every consent discussion includes the reasonable alternatives (Table 4.2). For uncomplicated symptomatic gallstones, waiting is a genuine option. In a UK trial, quality of life over 18 months was no better with surgery than with conservative management, and about 1 in 4 patients who chose to wait needed an operation within that time [6]. Patients who prefer to wait need to know which symptoms mean they should seek help urgently.

**STEP 2 Preparation, medicines, and recovery**

## 4.4 Before the operation

- **Tests.** Blood count, liver function tests, and an ultrasound scan are routine. Abnormal liver tests or a dilated bile duct suggest stones in the duct; these are investigated (for example, with magnetic resonance cholangiography or endoscopic ultrasound) and cleared before or during the operation.

<!-- -->

- **Medicines.** Blood thinners, diabetes medicines, and weight-loss injections need individual plans (Chapters 18, 22, and 23). Tell the patient not to stop any medicine unless instructed.

- **Fasting and smoking.** Use the standard advice in Chapter 2: a light meal up to 6 hours and clear fluids until 2 hours before anesthesia unless the anesthesia team says otherwise, and stop smoking now.

- **Pregnancy.** Women who could be pregnant are offered a pregnancy test on the day of surgery.

- **Day surgery.** Most planned operations are day cases. Confirm that an adult will take the patient home and stay overnight, and that they can reach the hospital if a problem develops.

- **While waiting.** A lower-fat diet may reduce attacks for some patients. Give the warning signs of complications (fever, jaundice, or pain that does not settle) and a number to call.

## 4.5 The day of surgery and discharge

Explain what the patient will feel on waking: soreness at the small wounds, a bloated feeling, and often pain in the right shoulder for a day or two, caused by gas under the diaphragm. Pain medicines are given on a schedule, sips of fluid start soon after the operation, and light food follows the same day. Nurses help the patient to walk within a few hours.

Before discharge, the patient should be awake and steady on their feet, have pain controlled with tablets, be drinking without vomiting, and have passed urine. Complete the personal instruction card (Appendix A), confirm the warning signs with teach-back, and make sure the patient knows the number to call.

Air travel is a frequent question in the Gulf, where many patients travel soon after surgery. The UK Civil Aviation Authority advises avoiding flights for about 24 hours after keyhole surgery, because of residual gas, and for about 10 days after open abdominal surgery [8]. In practice, most patients are advised to wait until they are comfortable, walking well, and free of complications, which is usually about a week for a short flight. Long-haul travel in the first weeks needs a discussion about walking during the flight, fluids, and clot prevention, and travel insurers may set their own conditions.

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

The answers below are written to be said aloud. They are short, honest, and consistent with the patient guide.

**“Can I live normally without my gallbladder?”**

Yes. Your liver keeps making bile, and it flows straight into the bowel. Most people notice no difference in their digestion.

**“Will I need a special diet afterward?”**

No. Some people find that large, fatty meals cause bloating or loose stools for a few weeks. If that happens, eat less fat while it settles.

**“Why not just take out the stones and leave the gallbladder?”**

The gallbladder that made the stones usually makes more. Removing it is the reliable way to stop the attacks.

**“Can the stones be dissolved, or broken with a laser?”**

Dissolving tablets help only a few people with small stones, take many months, and the stones often come back. Breaking gallbladder stones with shock waves is rarely used now. No diet or herbal remedy has been shown to remove gallstones [1].

**“Will the stones come back?”**

Stones cannot form again in a gallbladder that has been removed. Rarely, a stone remains in, or later forms in, the bile duct. That is usually treated with an endoscopy, not another operation.

**“My pain is not the typical kind. Will the operation cure it?”**

Perhaps not. The operation is very good at stopping typical attacks, but bloating, heartburn, and vague discomfort often continue. In one large trial, about 4 in 10 patients with gallstones and abdominal pain still had some pain a year later [2]. Let us look for other causes before deciding.

**“What happens if I wait?”**

For most people with uncomplicated stones, waiting is safe and reasonable. Some never have another attack. In one trial, about 1 in 4 people who chose to wait needed the operation within 18 months [6]. Come in at once if you have fever, yellow eyes or skin, or pain that does not settle.

**“Why does my shoulder hurt after the operation?”**

The gas we use irritates the diaphragm, and the nerve there makes the pain feel as if it is in the shoulder. It usually goes within 1 to 3 days. Walking and regular pain medicine help.

**“I take a weight-loss injection. Is that related, and should I stop it?”**

Rapid weight loss makes gallstones more likely, and these medicines are linked with more gallbladder problems [9]. Do not stop or change the injection yourself. Tell us the name and the date of your last dose, and the anesthesia team will advise you (Chapter 23).

**“I am pregnant, or might be. Can I have the operation?”**

Tell us before any operation. If gallstones cause serious problems during pregnancy, keyhole removal can be done safely by an experienced team, and many teams prefer the middle three months for a planned operation [10].

**“Why is my gallbladder sent to the laboratory?”**

Every gallbladder is examined under a microscope. Almost all results are normal. Very occasionally, about 6 or 7 times in 1,000 operations, the report shows an early cancer that was not suspected before [11]. We will tell you the result and what it means.

**“Can I fast in Ramadan around the operation?”**

Planned operations can usually be arranged before or after Ramadan if you prefer. After the operation, wait to fast until you are eating normally and no longer need regular medicines, usually 1 to 2 weeks. Illness is a recognized reason to delay fasting; ask your religious adviser if you are unsure.

**“Will I have scars?”**

You will have three or four small scars, the longest about 1 to 2 cm near the belly button. They fade over several months.

**What is the gallbladder?**

The gallbladder is a small bag under your liver. It stores bile, a liquid that helps you digest fat. Stones can form inside it. A stone can block the bag and cause strong pain, swelling, or infection.

You can live a normal life without a gallbladder. Your liver still makes bile. The bile flows straight into your gut instead of being stored.

**Why do I need this operation?**

Taking out the gallbladder stops gallstone attacks. It also prevents problems such as an infected gallbladder, yellow skin (jaundice), and swelling of the pancreas. The operation is very good at stopping typical attacks. It may not help bloating or indigestion.

**What happens in the operation?**

- You will be fully asleep.

- The surgeon makes 3 or 4 small cuts in your tummy. The biggest one is near your belly button.

- Gas is used to make space so the surgeon can see with a small camera. The gallbladder comes out through one of the cuts.

- The operation takes about 1 hour. Most people go home the same day or the next day.

- Sometimes the surgeon needs to make a bigger cut, or to leave a small tube (drain), to finish safely. This happens to a few people out of every 100.

**Before your operation**

- Stop smoking now.

- Tell us about all your medicines, including blood thinners, injections for diabetes or weight loss, and herbal medicines. Do not stop any medicine unless we tell you to.

- Follow the eating and drinking times we give you. Keep drinking water until 2 hours before, unless we tell you otherwise.

- Ask an adult to take you home and stay with you for the first night.

**1. Pain and pain medicines**

- Your tummy will be sore for a few days.

- Many people have pain in the shoulder for 1 to 3 days. It comes from the gas. Walking helps it go.

- Take your pain medicines regularly for the first few days, as we told you.

- Strong pain medicines can make you sleepy and constipated. Do not drive while you take them.

**2. Your wounds**

- Your small cuts are closed with glue, stitches, or clips. Keep the dressings clean and dry for 2 days.

- Some bruising is normal.

- Stitches under the skin dissolve on their own. If you have clips or stitches to take out, the date is on your card.

**3. Eating, drinking, and your bowels**

- Start with light meals. You can eat normally when you feel ready.

- You do not need a special diet. If fatty food upsets you, eat less fat for a few weeks.

- Your stools may be looser for a few weeks. This usually settles.

- Pain medicines can cause constipation. Drink water, eat fruit and vegetables, and walk.

**4. Rest, washing, and clothes**

- You may feel tired for 1 to 2 weeks. Rest, but get up and walk often.

- You can shower after 2 days. Pat the wounds dry.

- Do not soak in a bath or swim until the wounds have healed, usually about 2 weeks.

- Wear loose clothes that do not rub the wounds.

**5. Getting back to normal**

These are usual times. Your own plan is on your card.
