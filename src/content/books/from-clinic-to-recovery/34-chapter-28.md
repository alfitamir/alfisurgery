# Chapter 28 — Resuming Intimacy After Surgery

Patients rarely ask about sex and intimacy unless the clinician raises the subject first. Surgery can affect intimacy through pain, fatigue, altered body image, scars, stomas, nerve injury, and simple fear of causing harm, yet this concern is often left entirely unaddressed. Raising it briefly and matter-of-factly, as part of ordinary discharge counseling, gives patients permission to ask questions they would otherwise keep to themselves.

## 28.2 General principles

- **Pain and comfort guide timing** more than a fixed date. Most people can resume intimacy when they feel comfortable, are off strong pain medicines that cause drowsiness, and the wound is no longer tender to pressure.

<!-- -->

- **Avoid direct pressure or strain on a fresh wound.** Choosing positions that avoid weight on the operated area, and a gentle pace at first, reduces discomfort.

- **Abdominal and pelvic surgery** generally need a longer interval than minor procedures; follow the specific chapter for the operation performed.

- **A stoma** does not prevent intimacy; emptying the pouch beforehand and using a smaller pouch or cover often helps confidence (Chapter 14).

- **Breast and gynecologic surgery** can affect body image and sensation; patience, communication with a partner, and time usually help, and specific chapters note where nerve-related changes are possible (Chapter 10).

- **Hernia repair** rarely affects intimacy once the wound is comfortable, usually within one to two weeks (Chapter 6).

**STEP 4 Questions patients ask**

**“When can we be intimate again?”**

As a general guide, when you feel comfortable, are no longer taking strong pain medicines that make you drowsy, and the wound is no longer tender to pressure. This varies by operation; see your procedure chapter for specifics.

**“Will it damage my wound or my stitches?”**

Gentle activity that avoids direct pressure on the wound is very unlikely to cause harm. If you are unsure, ask your surgical team for guidance specific to your operation.

**“I have a stoma. Will my partner be able to cope?”**

Many couples adjust well, especially when the partner is included and informed. Emptying the pouch beforehand and using a cover can help your confidence (Chapter 14).

**“I feel differently about my body since the operation. Is that normal?”**

Yes, this is common and can take time to resolve. Talking with your partner, and with a counselor if it would help, often makes a real difference.
