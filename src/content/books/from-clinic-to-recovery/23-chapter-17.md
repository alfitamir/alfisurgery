# Chapter 17 — Splenectomy and Living Without a Spleen

The operation to remove the spleen is usually straightforward, often by keyhole surgery, and recovery is quick. The counseling is not about the operation alone. A person without a working spleen carries a lifelong risk of overwhelming infection, which is uncommon but can become fatal within hours. Most of these infections can be prevented by vaccination, antibiotics, and prompt action when fever develops. The surgeon is often the person best placed to start this lifelong protection, and the patient guide in this chapter is one that patients should keep for life. In regions where sickle cell disease and thalassemia are common, including the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia, these conversations are frequent and often involve children and their families.

**STEP 1 Explaining the reasons and the operation**

## 17.1 Why the spleen may be removed

The reasons for splenectomy, and the alternatives, depend on the condition (Figure 17.1). In immune thrombocytopenia, newer platelet-stimulating medicines and rituximab are now alternatives to splenectomy, and guidelines suggest delaying splenectomy for at least a year after diagnosis because some patients recover [1]. In hereditary spherocytosis, thalassemia, and sickle cell disease, splenectomy may be considered for an enlarged or overactive spleen, and partial splenectomy may suit some children. After injury, most hemodynamically stable patients are managed without an operation, sometimes with embolization of the splenic artery [2].

**STEP 2 Preparation, lifelong protection, and recovery**

## 17.3 Vaccination

For planned splenectomy, vaccines should be given at least two weeks before the operation; after an emergency splenectomy, they are given about two weeks afterward, before discharge if possible [3]. Patients need vaccination against pneumococcus, Haemophilus influenzae type b, and meningococcus (groups A, C, W, Y and group B), with boosters and annual influenza vaccination, according to the national schedule [3]. Record what was given, and give the patient a written record.

## 17.4 Lifelong infection protection

Figure 17.2 shows the five protections every patient should understand. Daily antibiotic prophylaxis is offered, at least for the first years after splenectomy and for life in patients at higher risk; all patients should also hold a supply of standby antibiotics to start immediately if fever or other signs of infection develop, and seek medical help at once [3]. Patients should carry an alert card, inform every clinician that they have no spleen, take malaria prevention seriously when traveling to endemic areas, and seek antibiotics after any animal bite [3].

## 17.5 Before and after the operation

- **Before.** Vaccines given at least two weeks earlier; blood count and blood products planned according to the condition; for immune thrombocytopenia, platelet support or medicines as advised by the hematologist.

<!-- -->

- **Clot prevention.** Clots in the portal or splenic vein can occur after splenectomy, especially with a large spleen or certain blood disorders. Follow the unit’s thromboprophylaxis plan.

- **After.** Walk early, use breathing exercises, and report left shoulder or upper abdominal pain, fever, or vomiting.

- **Platelet count.** The platelet count often rises after splenectomy; the team will check it and advise on any treatment.

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

**“Can I live without my spleen?”**

Yes. Other organs take over most of its work. The main change is a higher risk of certain serious infections, which you can greatly reduce with vaccines, antibiotics, and quick action for fever.

**“Will I always be getting infections?”**

No. Most people do not get more everyday infections. The concern is a rare but serious infection that can develop quickly, which is why we ask you to act fast if you have a fever.

**“Do I need antibiotics forever?”**

We usually advise a daily antibiotic for at least the first years, and for life in some people. Everyone should keep a standby course at home [3].

**“What should I do if I get a fever?”**

Take your standby antibiotic at once, and see a doctor the same day. Tell them you have no spleen and show your card.

**“Can I go for Umrah or Hajj?”**

Yes. Make sure your vaccines are up to date, including meningococcal vaccine, take your antibiotics with you, and seek help early if you feel unwell.

**“Can I travel to countries with malaria?”**

Malaria is more dangerous without a spleen. If possible, avoid these areas; if you must go, get specialist travel advice, take preventive tablets, and use mosquito protection.

**“Can I keep pets?”**

Yes, but any bite or scratch from an animal needs antibiotics. See a doctor the same day.

**“Will my platelets stay normal after surgery?”**

For many people with immune thrombocytopenia, splenectomy brings lasting improvement, but not for everyone. Your hematologist will follow your counts [1].

**“My child needs the operation. Is it different for children?”**

Children have a higher risk of infection without a spleen, so vaccines and antibiotics are especially important. For some conditions, removing only part of the spleen is possible. We will plan with the pediatric team (Chapter 25).

**Why this matters**

Your spleen helped fight some infections. Without it, these infections can become serious very fast. You can protect yourself with five simple steps, for the rest of your life.

**3. If I get a fever**

- **Fever of 38 °C or more, shivering, or feeling suddenly very unwell is an emergency.**

- Take your standby antibiotic at once.

- Go to a doctor or emergency department the same day. Say: “I have no spleen.”

**4. My alert card**

- Carry your alert card in your wallet or phone case at all times.

- Tell every doctor, dentist, and pharmacist that you have no spleen.

**5. Travel and animals**

- Before travel, check your vaccines and take your antibiotics with you.

- Malaria is more dangerous without a spleen. Get specialist advice before visiting malaria areas.

- For Umrah or Hajj, make sure your meningococcal vaccine is up to date.

- Any animal bite or scratch: see a doctor the same day for antibiotics.
