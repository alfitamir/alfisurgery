# Chapter 20 — Heart Disease, High Blood Pressure, and Surgery

Heart attacks, heart failure, and abnormal rhythms are leading causes of serious complications and death after non-cardiac surgery. Patients with known heart disease, coronary stents, pacemakers, or high blood pressure are often anxious about anesthesia, and families may fear that surgery will “strain the heart.” The surgeon’s task is to identify patients who need assessment before surgery, to avoid unnecessary tests that delay treatment, to manage heart medicines safely, and to explain the risks clearly. Coronary disease, diabetes, and smoking are common in the Gulf, often at a younger age, so these conversations are frequent.

**STEP 1 Assessing the heart and explaining the plan**

## 20.1 A stepwise cardiac assessment

Current guidelines recommend a stepwise approach (Figure 20.1) [1, 2]. Emergency surgery proceeds without delay. Active heart problems, such as unstable chest pain, decompensated heart failure, serious arrhythmias, or severe valve disease, are treated first when time allows. For other patients, the risk of the operation is combined with patient factors, such as those in the Revised Cardiac Risk Index: high-risk surgery, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, insulin-treated diabetes, and impaired kidney function [3]. Patients who can climb two flights of stairs without stopping usually need no further testing. Tests such as cardiac biomarkers, echocardiography, or stress testing are ordered only when the result would change management [1, 2].

## 20.2 Coronary stents and the timing of surgery

Surgery soon after coronary stenting carries a higher risk of stent thrombosis and heart attack, highest in the first weeks and months. The 2024 American guideline recommends delaying elective non-cardiac surgery for at least 14 days after balloon angioplasty and at least 30 days after any stent, ideally 6 months after a drug-eluting stent placed for stable disease, and 12 months after a stent placed for an acute coronary syndrome [1] (Table 20.2). Decisions about antiplatelet medicines must be made with the cardiologist (Chapter 22). Patients should never stop antiplatelet medicines on their own before surgery.

**Table 20.2.** _Timing of elective surgery after coronary intervention, based on the 2024 ACC/AHA guideline._

| **After**                                       | **Usual minimum before elective surgery** | **Notes**                                                              |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Balloon angioplasty without a stent**         | At least 14 days                          |                                                                        |
| **Bare-metal stent**                            | At least 30 days                          |                                                                        |
| **Drug-eluting stent for stable disease**       | Ideally 6 months; never within 30 days    | Time-sensitive surgery may be considered earlier with the cardiologist |
| **Stent for a heart attack or unstable angina** | Ideally 12 months                         | Time-sensitive surgery is a joint decision                             |

## 20.3 Heart medicines around surgery

Beta-blockers that patients already take should be continued; starting a high-dose beta-blocker shortly before surgery increased stroke and death in a large trial and should be avoided [4]. Statins are continued. For angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers, the 2024 ACC/AHA guideline rates omitting the dose 24 hours before surgery as reasonable to consider (may be beneficial) in selected patients with controlled blood pressure having higher-risk surgery, while continuing them is reasonable in patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction [1]; a 2024 trial found no difference in major complications between continuing and stopping these medicines 48 hours before major surgery, although continuing them was associated with more low blood pressure during anesthesia [5]. This is not a flat rule either way: follow the hospital protocol, and consider the patient’s heart failure status. Table 20.3 summarizes the usual approach.

**Table 20.3.** _Usual approach to heart medicines before surgery. Always follow the hospital protocol._

| **Medicine**                                         | **Usual approach before surgery**                                                                                                                                                       | **Notes**                                                       |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Beta-blockers**                                    | Continue, including on the morning of surgery                                                                                                                                           | Do not start on the day of surgery [4]                          |
| **Statins**                                          | Continue                                                                                                                                                                                |                                                                 |
| **ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers** | May be omitted 24 hours before higher-risk surgery in selected patients with controlled blood pressure, according to protocol; continue in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction | Not a flat rule either way; watch for low blood pressure [1, 5] |
| **Diuretics**                                        | Often omitted on the morning of surgery                                                                                                                                                 | Restart when drinking normally                                  |
| **Nitrates and calcium channel blockers**            | Continue                                                                                                                                                                                |                                                                 |
| **Antiplatelets and anticoagulants**                 | Individual plan with the cardiologist                                                                                                                                                   | See Chapter 22                                                  |
| **SGLT2 inhibitors (for heart failure)**             | Stop before surgery                                                                                                                                                                     | See Chapter 18                                                  |

## 20.4 Blood pressure on the day of surgery

Many patients arrive anxious with a raised blood pressure. Mild to moderate hypertension is not a reason to postpone surgery; very high readings, around 180/110 mmHg or above, or high blood pressure with symptoms or organ damage, need assessment before elective surgery [2]. Patients should take their usual blood pressure medicines as instructed, and newly found hypertension should be referred for long-term treatment.

## 20.5 Heart failure and valve disease

Patients with heart failure should be at their best fluid balance before elective surgery, and those with symptoms that have recently worsened need review first. A new murmur, unexplained breathlessness, or fainting suggests possible valve disease, particularly aortic stenosis, and should be assessed with echocardiography before major elective surgery [2].

## 20.6 Pacemakers and defibrillators

Patients should bring their device card. The device should have been checked within the recommended interval before surgery. Electrical diathermy can interfere with these devices; defibrillator shock therapies are usually suspended during surgery with external defibrillation pads in place, and pacemakers may be reprogrammed for patients who depend on them. The device is checked again after surgery when it has been reprogrammed or exposed to interference [6].

**STEP 2 Preparation and recovery**

## 20.7 Before and after the operation

- **Bring** all heart medicines, the device card, and any recent heart test results.

<!-- -->

- **Morning of surgery.** Take the medicines on the written plan, with a sip of water.

- **After surgery.** Heart monitoring and, in high-risk patients, blood tests for heart strain (troponin) may be used according to protocol, because heart injury after surgery is often silent [2].

- **Pain and anxiety.** Good pain relief reduces strain on the heart.

- **Mobilize early**, and restart usual heart medicines as soon as possible.

- **Atrial fibrillation** can begin after surgery; report palpitations.

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

**“Do I need heart tests before my operation?”**

Only if they would change what we do. If you can climb two flights of stairs without stopping, you often need no extra tests [1].

**“I had a stent recently. Can I have my operation?”**

For a planned operation, it is usually safer to wait, ideally 6 months after a stent for stable disease or 12 months after a heart attack. We will decide with your cardiologist [1].

**“Should I stop my aspirin or other blood thinner?”**

Never stop them yourself. We will make a plan with your cardiologist (Chapter 22).

**“Should I take my heart tablets on the morning of surgery?”**

Most, yes, especially beta-blockers. Some blood pressure tablets and water tablets may be stopped that morning. Follow your written plan.

**“My blood pressure is high today. Will you cancel?”**

Usually not. Many people are nervous on the day. Only very high readings need us to pause and check [2].

**“I have a pacemaker. Is the operation safe?”**

Yes. Bring your device card. We will check it and adjust it if needed during the operation [6].

**“Can I fast in Ramadan with my heart medicines?”**

Many people can, with the doses re-timed. Discuss it with your cardiologist, and do not fast until you have recovered from surgery.

**“Does shisha affect my heart?”**

Yes. Shisha and cigarettes both strain the heart and blood vessels. Stopping helps your heart and your recovery.

**Before your operation**

**After your operation**

- You may have heart monitoring and blood tests.

- Tell the nurse at once about chest pain, breathlessness, or palpitations.

- Walk as soon as you can.
