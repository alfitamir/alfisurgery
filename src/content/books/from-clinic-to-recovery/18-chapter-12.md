# Chapter 12 — Salivary Gland Surgery

Salivary gland surgery brings one fear to the front of every consultation: “Will my face be paralysed?” The facial nerve runs through the parotid gland, and nerves to the lower lip, tongue, and tongue movement lie close to the submandibular gland. Most salivary lumps are benign, and most nerve weakness after surgery recovers, but patients need honest figures, clear eye-care instructions if weakness occurs, and an explanation of the less familiar after-effects, such as a numb earlobe and sweating on eating. This chapter covers parotid and submandibular surgery and gland-preserving treatment of salivary stones.

**STEP 1 Explaining the condition and the operation**

## 12.1 Assessing a salivary gland lump

A salivary lump is assessed with ultrasound and a needle sample, with MRI when the lump is deep, fixed, or suspicious, or when the nerve may be involved [1]. Most parotid lumps are benign. A pleomorphic adenoma is usually removed, because it continues to grow and carries a small risk of becoming malignant over many years. A Warthin tumor, which is benign and common in older smokers, can be observed or removed according to the patient’s symptoms and preference [1]. Signs that suggest cancer, such as pain, rapid growth, facial weakness, or fixation, need multidisciplinary review. Figure 12.1 summarizes the pathway.

## 12.3 Submandibular gland and salivary stones

Stones and repeated infection are the most common reasons for submandibular surgery. Where possible, stones are removed with gland-preserving techniques, either through the mouth or with sialendoscopy, a fine telescope passed into the duct. When symptoms persist, or for a tumor, the gland is removed through a neck incision. Three nerves need to be discussed: the branch of the facial nerve to the lower lip, the lingual nerve supplying sensation to the tongue, and the hypoglossal nerve that moves the tongue.

## 12.4 Salivary gland cancer

Malignant salivary tumors are uncommon and varied, and are managed by a multidisciplinary head and neck team [1]. Surgery may include neck dissection, and radiotherapy is often added. If the facial nerve is directly involved by cancer, part of it may need to be removed, with immediate or later reconstruction and procedures to protect the eye and restore movement. These conversations need extra time and the support of a specialist nurse (Section 1.7).

**STEP 2 Preparation, medicines, and recovery**

## 12.5 Before the operation

- **Facial nerve function.** Examine and record facial movement before surgery, using the five movements in Figure 12.2.

<!-- -->

- **Medicines and fasting.** Use the standard plans in Chapters 2, 18, 22, and 23.

- **Smoking.** Encourage stopping, particularly for patients with Warthin tumors.

- **Shaving.** Men may need to shave the area in front of the ear on the day; advise against shaving over the scar for some weeks afterward.

## 12.6 After the operation

A small drain is often left overnight. Facial movement is checked on waking and before discharge. If the eye does not close fully, the cornea must be protected with lubricating drops by day, ointment and a shield or tape at night, and review if the eye becomes red or painful. Numbness of the earlobe is expected after parotidectomy and usually improves over months. A soft swelling of saliva under the skin (sialocele) can occur in the first weeks and is usually managed with aspiration and pressure.

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

**“Will my face be paralysed?”**

Weakness of part of the face after parotid surgery happens in about 1 or 2 in every 10 people, and in most it recovers over weeks to months. Permanent weakness is uncommon, about 3 to 4 in 100 after surgery for benign lumps in published series [2, 3].

**“If my face is weak, will it come back?”**

Usually, yes. In one large study, 9 in 10 people with temporary weakness had normal movement again within 6 months [2].

**“Is it cancer?”**

Most parotid lumps are not cancer. The needle sample and scan help us tell, and the laboratory gives the final answer after the operation.

**“If it is benign, why remove it?”**

This type of lump slowly grows and, over many years, a small number change. It is simpler and safer to remove while small [1].

**“Can I just keep an eye on it?”**

For some benign lumps, such as a Warthin tumor, yes. We can check it with ultrasound if you prefer not to have surgery [1].

**“Why is my earlobe numb?”**

A small skin nerve to the earlobe is often stretched or divided. The numbness usually becomes smaller over months.

**“Why do I sweat on my cheek when I eat?”**

This is called Frey syndrome. Nerves that used to make saliva regrow into the skin’s sweat glands. It is often mild; if it bothers you, injections can control it.

**“Will my mouth be dry?”**

No. You have several other salivary glands, which make more than enough saliva.

**“Can the stone be removed without removing the gland?”**

Often, yes. Many stones can be removed through the mouth or with a small telescope, keeping the gland.

**“Can I pray and do ablution?”**

Yes, from the first day. Keep the wound dry for 1 to 2 days, using a waterproof dressing.

**1. Pain and pain medicines**

- The area will be sore for a few days. Take your pain medicines regularly at first, as we told you.

- The first bite of a meal may cause a brief ache in the cheek. This usually settles.

**2. Your wound**

- Keep the dressing dry for 1 to 2 days. Use a waterproof dressing for ablution.

- Your earlobe and the skin near the scar may feel numb. This usually gets better over months.

- Men: do not shave over the scar for a few weeks. The skin may be numb, so take care.

**3. Your face and your eye**

- Check your face in a mirror: raise your eyebrows, close your eyes tightly, smile, puff your cheeks, and pull down your lower lip.

- Some weakness is common and usually recovers over weeks to months.

- If your eye does not close fully: use eye drops during the day, ointment and a shield or tape at night, and call us.

**4. Eating and drinking**

- Start with soft food and eat normally when ready.

- Some people later notice sweating on the cheek when eating. Tell us if it bothers you; it can be treated.
