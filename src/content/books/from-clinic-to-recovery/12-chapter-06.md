# Chapter 06 — Groin Hernia Repair: Inguinal and Femoral Hernias

Groin hernia repair is one of the most common operations in the world, and most patients meet the surgeon first in the outpatient clinic, often with a painless lump and a long list of questions. Does it need an operation at all? Is the mesh safe? Keyhole or open? When can I lift, pray, or go back to work? The answers have changed over the last decade: watchful waiting is now an accepted option for some men, mesh repair is the standard, and long periods of restricted activity are no longer advised. This chapter applies the five-step method to inguinal and femoral hernias, with a short section on umbilical and epigastric hernias.

**STEP 1 Explaining the hernia and the operation**

## 6.1 Who needs repair, and how soon

Most groin hernias slowly enlarge and become more uncomfortable, but strangulation is uncommon. For men with an inguinal hernia that causes few or no symptoms, watchful waiting is a safe option. In a randomized trial, acute incarceration occurred in about 1.8 per 1,000 patient-years, and about a quarter of men in the waiting group had chosen surgery by 2 years [1]. With longer follow-up, however, about 68 in 100 had crossed over to repair by 10 years, and about 79 in 100 of men older than 65, most often because of pain [2]. Patients who choose to wait should know that they will probably need the operation later.

Women and patients with a femoral hernia are different. Femoral hernias carry a higher risk of strangulation and should be repaired promptly; in women, a femoral hernia is easily mistaken for an inguinal one, and a keyhole approach, which allows both openings to be inspected, is preferred [3, 4]. A hernia that is hard, painful, and cannot be pushed back needs emergency assessment. Figure 6.1 summarizes the decision.

## 6.3 Choosing the technique

Mesh repair is recommended for adult inguinal hernias, and both the open Lichtenstein operation and the keyhole operations (totally extraperitoneal or transabdominal preperitoneal repair) are supported by strong evidence [3]. When the surgeon has the expertise, keyhole repair causes less early pain and less chronic pain, and allows a faster return to activity; open repair can be done under local anesthesia and avoids general anesthesia [3, 4]. Table 6.2 helps patients compare the two. For patients who decline mesh, a tissue repair such as the Shouldice operation is an option in experienced hands.

**Table 6.2.** *Keyhole and open groin hernia repair compared, for use with patients.*

|                             | **Keyhole repair**                                         | **Open repair**                                                                             |
|-----------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Cuts**                    | Three small cuts                                           | One cut of about 6 to 8 cm in the groin                                                     |
| **Anesthesia**              | General                                                    | Local, regional, or general                                                                 |
| **Early pain and recovery** | Usually less pain and a faster return                      | A little more pain in the first week                                                        |
| **Long-term groin pain**    | Less common                                                | More common, though usually mild                                                            |
| **Recurrence**              | Similar in experienced hands                               | Similar in experienced hands                                                                |
| **Particularly suits**      | Hernias on both sides, recurrence after open repair, women | Patients unfit for general anesthesia, previous lower abdominal surgery, patient preference |

## 6.4 Umbilical and epigastric hernias: what changes

The same principles apply to hernias at or above the belly button, with three differences. First, a mesh is recommended for most repairs because it lowers recurrence, and most can be repaired by an open approach with a flat mesh placed behind the muscle; keyhole repair is considered for large defects or when the risk of wound problems is high [5]. Second, a later pregnancy increases the risk of recurrence, so women who plan a pregnancy can discuss whether to delay repair if symptoms allow [6]. Third, smoking, obesity, and poorly controlled diabetes increase wound problems and recurrence, and should be addressed before a planned repair. Incisional hernias after previous surgery are often more complex and need individual planning.

**STEP 2 Preparation, medicines, and recovery**

## 6.5 Before the operation

- **Anesthesia.** Discuss whether local, regional, or general anesthesia suits the patient and the technique.

<!-- -->

- **Medicines and fasting.** Use the standard plans for blood thinners, diabetes medicines, and weight-loss injections (Chapters 18, 22, and 23) and the fasting advice in Chapter 2.

- **Smoking and weight.** Stopping smoking reduces wound problems. For umbilical and incisional hernias, weight loss before surgery is often advised.

- **Straining.** Ask about chronic cough, constipation, and urinary symptoms. Men with prostate symptoms have a higher risk of difficulty passing urine after surgery.

- **Hair.** Patients should not shave the groin at home; if hair removal is needed, it is done with clippers in hospital.

- **Day surgery.** Confirm an adult escort and overnight support.

## 6.6 The day of surgery and discharge

Most patients go home the same day. Before discharge they should be walking, have pain controlled with tablets, and have passed urine. Explain that bruising often spreads into the scrotum or labia over the first few days, and that close-fitting underwear makes swelling more comfortable. Complete the personal instruction card (Appendix A) and confirm the warning signs with teach-back.

## 6.7 Returning to activity

Advice about rest after hernia repair has traditionally been cautious, with weeks of lifting restrictions. The evidence does not support this. International guidelines advise patients to return to normal activities as soon as they feel comfortable, and no study has shown that early activity increases recurrence [3]. In a large study of patients advised to resume activity after one day, the median time off work was 7 days, and reoperation rates were no higher than in other patients [7]. Table 6.3 and Figure 6.2 give typical times, which patients can shorten or lengthen according to comfort.

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

**“Is my hernia dangerous? What happens if I wait?”**

For most men with a hernia that causes little trouble, waiting is safe. The risk of the hernia getting stuck is low, about 2 in 1,000 each year [1]. But most people who wait have the operation eventually, about 7 in 10 within 10 years [2]. If you are a woman, or if it is a femoral hernia, we advise repair soon.

**“Is the mesh safe? I have read bad things about mesh.”**

The problems you read about were mainly with mesh used in pelvic surgery for women. Hernia mesh has been used safely for decades, and guidelines recommend it because it greatly lowers the chance of the hernia coming back [3]. Serious problems with it are rare.

**“Can you repair it without mesh?”**

Yes, a stitched repair is possible, but the hernia is more likely to come back. If you prefer it, we will discuss it in detail.

**“Keyhole or open: which is better for me?”**

Both are good operations. Keyhole usually means less pain and a quicker return to activity, and it is best for hernias on both sides, for women, and for hernias that came back after open surgery. Open surgery can be done under local anesthesia. We will choose together.

**“Can I stay awake?”**

Open repair can usually be done under local anesthesia, with medicine to help you relax. Keyhole repair needs a general anesthetic.

**“When can I lift heavy things and go back to the gym?”**

As soon as you are comfortable. Lifting early does not make the hernia come back [3]. Start gently and let pain guide you.

**“Will it come back?”**

With a mesh repair, it comes back in a small number of people, about [X] in 100 in our unit. Smoking, heavy coughing, and straining raise the risk.

**“Why is my scrotum (or groin) so bruised and swollen?”**

Blood and fluid from the operation track down with gravity. It looks alarming but usually settles within 2 to 3 weeks. Firm, supportive underwear helps.

**“Will it affect my sex life or fertility?”**

For almost everyone, no. The tube that carries sperm runs through the area and is protected during the operation; injury is rare. A few men notice discomfort with ejaculation for a while.

**“Why is the skin near my scar numb?”**

Small skin nerves are stretched or divided during open surgery. The numbness usually improves over months.

**“I have hernias on both sides. Can you fix both at once?”**

Yes. Keyhole surgery allows both sides to be repaired through the same small cuts [3].

**“Can I pray and fast normally?”**

You can pray from the first day; sit if bowing or kneeling is uncomfortable. You can fast again once you are eating normally, usually within a few days.

**“I have an umbilical hernia and plan to have more children. Should I wait?”**

A pregnancy after repair raises the chance of the hernia coming back [6]. If your hernia is not troubling you, we can discuss repairing it after your family is complete.

**What is a groin hernia?**

The muscle wall of your groin has a natural weak spot. A hernia is a gap there. Fat or a loop of bowel pushes through and makes a lump. The lump is often bigger when you stand, cough, or strain, and smaller when you lie down.

**Why repair it?**

A hernia does not heal on its own. It usually grows slowly and becomes more uncomfortable. Rarely, the bowel gets stuck in it, which is an emergency. The operation closes the gap.

**What happens in the operation?**

- The surgeon closes the gap and covers it with a mesh. This is a thin, soft net. Your body grows into it and makes the wall strong.

- **Keyhole repair:** 3 small cuts. You will be fully asleep.

- **Open repair:** 1 cut in the groin. You may be awake with numbing medicine, or asleep.

- The operation takes about 1 hour. Most people go home the same day.

**Before your operation**

- Stop smoking now.

- Tell us about all your medicines, including blood thinners and injections for diabetes or weight loss. Do not stop any medicine unless we tell you to.

- Follow the eating and drinking times we give you.

- Do not shave your groin. We will clip hair if needed.

- Ask an adult to take you home and stay with you for the first night.

**1. Pain and pain medicines**

- Your groin will be sore for a few days. Take your pain medicines regularly at first, as we told you.

- Strong pain medicines can make you sleepy and constipated. Do not drive while you take them.

- Hold a pillow against the wound when you cough or laugh.

**2. Your wounds**

- Keep the dressings clean and dry for 2 days.

- Bruising often spreads into the scrotum, labia, or thigh. It looks worse than it is and settles in 2 to 3 weeks.

- Firm, supportive underwear makes swelling more comfortable.

- A soft swelling where the hernia was may be fluid. It usually goes on its own.

**3. Eating, drinking, and your bowels**

- Eat and drink normally when you feel ready.

- Avoid constipation: drink water, eat fruit and vegetables, and walk. Straining is uncomfortable.

**4. Rest, washing, and clothes**

- Get up and walk from the first day.

- You can shower after 2 days. Pat the wounds dry.

- Do not soak in a bath or swim until the wounds have healed.

**5. Getting back to normal**

Let comfort guide you. Being active early does not make the hernia come back. Your own plan is on your card.
