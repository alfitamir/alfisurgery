# Preface

A technically perfect operation can still leave a patient confused, frightened, or unprepared. The patient may not have understood why the operation was needed, what the alternatives were, or what to do during the first week at home. Outcomes depend on technique, but they also depend on whether the patient understood the plan, trusted the team, and was able to carry the plan out.

Much of that understanding, and most of that trust, is built long before the operating room. It begins in the outpatient clinic, where surgeon and patient meet for the first time, and it continues in the short conversations that follow on the ward, at discharge, and on the telephone. These conversations are often rushed, and they are rarely taught. This book treats them as a clinical skill that can be learned, structured, and improved, in the same way as a surgical technique.

The book follows the patient along the whole perioperative journey: the first consultation, the consent discussion, preparation for the operation, the hospital stay, recovery at home, and the return to normal life. At each step it gives surgeons, residents, and nurses the words, the structure, and the tools to explain clearly, to answer questions honestly, and to give the same safe advice every time.

Part I sets out the foundations: how to run a consultation and obtain valid consent, how to explain enhanced recovery so that patients take an active part in it, and how to support spoken explanation with printed and digital material. Each procedure chapter in Parts II and III then follows the same five steps: a counseling script, a preparation and recovery pathway, answers to the questions patients ask most often, a risk and consent checklist, and a patient guide that can be handed over as written. Separate chapters address patients with diabetes or heart and lung disease, patients taking anticoagulants or the newer weight-loss injections, older adults, children, and others who need adjusted advice.

The book is written in plain English for clinicians who work in busy outpatient clinics and wards. It draws on international surgical guidance and on the daily realities of a hospital in the Arabian Gulf, where families are closely involved in care and where questions about fasting, prayer, travel, and modesty are a normal part of counseling. Questions like these arise, in different forms, in every setting. I hope this book helps readers anywhere to explain surgery clearly, to answer patients’ questions with confidence, and to send every patient home safe and informed.

_Tamer Rushdy Elalfy_
