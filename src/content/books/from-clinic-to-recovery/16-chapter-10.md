# Chapter 10 — Breast Cancer Surgery

A diagnosis of breast cancer changes a patient’s life in a single conversation. The surgeon is often the first specialist she meets, and the way the diagnosis and the choices are explained shapes how she copes with everything that follows. Surgical decisions in breast cancer are rarely about survival alone: breast-conserving surgery and mastectomy give the same long-term survival, so choices about the breast, reconstruction, and the armpit depend heavily on what matters to the patient. This chapter focuses on the surgeon’s part of the conversation, within the multidisciplinary pathway. Systemic treatment and radiotherapy are described only as far as the surgeon needs to explain them.

**STEP 1 Explaining the diagnosis and the treatment**

## 10.1 Giving the diagnosis

Give the result in person, in a private room, with enough time and a specialist breast nurse present. Ask beforehand whom the patient would like with her. Use the SPIKES approach of Section 1.7: find out what she already suspects, ask how much detail she wants now, say the word “cancer” clearly, pause, and respond to her emotion before moving to the plan. Most patients remember little after the word “cancer,” so end with a short written summary, the name and number of her nurse, and the date of the next appointment.

## 10.2 The multidisciplinary plan

Every new breast cancer is discussed by a multidisciplinary team before treatment starts. Explain that the plan depends on the type of cancer (its hormone receptor and HER2 status), its size, and whether lymph nodes are involved. For most early cancers, surgery comes first. For some, particularly HER2-positive and triple-negative cancers, or larger cancers, drug treatment is given first; this can shrink the cancer, allow a smaller operation, and show how well the cancer responds. ## 10.3 Keeping the breast or removing it

Breast-conserving surgery followed by radiotherapy gives the same long-term survival as mastectomy for suitable early breast cancers, shown by randomized trials with 20 years of follow-up [1, 2]. Radiotherapy is part of breast-conserving treatment, not an optional extra. Mastectomy may be recommended when the cancer is large relative to the breast, involves several areas of the breast, or when radiotherapy is not possible; some patients choose it for personal reasons. A clear margin is defined as no cancer cells at the inked edge of the removed tissue, a standard that has reduced the need for re-operation [3]; patients should still be told that a second operation is sometimes needed.

|                        | **Breast-conserving surgery and radiotherapy**               | **Mastectomy**                                                                     |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

Some patients ask to have the other, healthy breast removed. For women without a high-risk gene mutation or strong family history, this does not improve survival, and the American Society of Breast Surgeons discourages it in average-risk women while supporting shared decision-making [4]. Explore the fear behind the request, and explain what removing the other breast can and cannot achieve.

## 10.4 Reconstruction

Every suitable patient having a mastectomy should be offered a discussion of reconstruction, even if she decides against it. Reconstruction can be immediate, at the time of mastectomy, or delayed until after other treatments. It may use an implant, the patient’s own tissue, or both. Some women prefer no reconstruction and use an external prosthesis, which is an equally valid choice. Explain that reconstruction usually needs more than one operation, that it restores shape but not normal sensation, and that radiotherapy can affect the result.

## 10.5 The armpit

Surgery to the armpit has become much less extensive. Patients with clinically and ultrasound-negative nodes normally have sentinel node biopsy, which removes only the first one to three nodes. For carefully selected postmenopausal women aged 50 or older with a small, low-grade, hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative cancer, a negative axillary ultrasound, and planned breast-conserving surgery with radiotherapy, sentinel node biopsy can now be omitted [5, 6, 7]. When one or two sentinel nodes contain cancer in a patient having breast-conserving surgery and radiotherapy, removing the rest of the nodes does not improve survival and is not recommended [5, 8]. Axillary dissection is still used for selected patients with more extensive nodal disease.

The main reason for this change is lymphedema, persistent swelling of the arm. In a pooled analysis, about 6 in 100 women developed lymphedema after sentinel node biopsy, compared with about 20 in 100 after axillary dissection [9]. ## 10.6 Genetics, fertility, and family

The American Society of Breast Surgeons recommends that genetic testing be made available to all patients with breast cancer [10]. A result can change surgical choices, and has implications for sisters, daughters, and other relatives, so it should be discussed with care and with genetic counseling where available. In communities where consanguineous marriage is common, family history should be taken in detail.

Young women should be asked about plans for children before any treatment that may affect fertility, and offered referral for fertility preservation as early as possible, ideally before chemotherapy starts [11]. Pregnancy, breastfeeding, and marriage are common concerns for younger patients; raise them openly, because many will not ask.

**STEP 2 Preparation, medicines, and recovery**

## 10.7 Before the operation

- **Localization.** For cancers that cannot be felt, a wire, seed, or marker is placed under imaging before or on the day of surgery. Explain the procedure and when it will happen.

<!-- -->

- **Sentinel node tracer.** A small injection is given before surgery to find the sentinel node. Some tracers color the urine or the skin of the breast for a time.

- **Medicines.** Use the standard plans for blood thinners, diabetes medicines, and weight-loss injections (Chapters 18, 22, and 23). Ask about herbal and traditional remedies.

- **Practical preparation.** A soft, front-fastening bra; loose clothes that open at the front; help at home for the first week after mastectomy.

- **Support.** Introduce the specialist breast nurse, written information, and patient support groups.

## 10.8 After the operation

Breast-conserving surgery with sentinel node biopsy is usually a day case. After mastectomy or axillary dissection, one or two drains may be left for several days, and the patient may go home with them. Fluid often collects under the skin (seroma) after drains are removed and may need draining in clinic. Gentle arm and shoulder exercises start the day after surgery to prevent stiffness, increasing as the drains come out. Numbness of the inner upper arm is common after axillary surgery. Explain how and when the pathology results will be discussed, because they determine whether further surgery or other treatment is needed.

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

**“Did I cause this?”**

Breast cancer is common, and most women who develop it have no single clear cause. It is not something you brought on yourself, and it is not a punishment.

**“Will removing the whole breast make me live longer?”**

No. For cancers like yours, keeping the breast with radiotherapy gives the same long-term survival as mastectomy [1, 2].

**“Why do I need radiotherapy if you remove the cancer?”**

Radiotherapy treats any cancer cells that may remain in the breast. It is what makes keeping the breast as safe as removing it.

**“Do you need to remove all my lymph nodes?”**

Usually not. Most women need only the first few nodes checked, and some do not need any removed [5].

**“Will my arm swell?”**

About 6 in 100 women have arm swelling after a sentinel node biopsy, and about 20 in 100 after all the nodes are removed [9]. We will teach you exercises and the early signs to watch for.

**“Should I have the other breast removed to be safe?”**

For women without a high-risk gene or strong family history, removing the healthy breast does not help you live longer [4]. Let us talk about what worries you most.

**“Will my daughters or sisters get it?”**

Most breast cancers are not inherited. We offer genetic testing, and if it shows an inherited cause, we can advise your relatives about their own risk [10].

**“Can I still have children?”**

Many women do. Some treatments can affect fertility, so if you may want children, we will refer you to a fertility specialist before treatment starts [11].

**“Can my husband or family be told first?”**

If you wish, yes. It is your decision who is told and how much. We will follow your wishes.

**“Can I use herbal or traditional remedies?”**

Please tell us about anything you take. Some remedies interact with treatment. None can replace surgery, radiotherapy, or medicines for cancer.

**“Can I have a female surgeon?”**

We will try to arrange this where possible, and a female nurse will always be present during examinations.
