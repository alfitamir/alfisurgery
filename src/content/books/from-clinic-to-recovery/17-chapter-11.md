# Chapter 11 — Thyroid and Parathyroid Surgery

Thyroid and parathyroid operations are performed in the neck, close to the nerves that control the voice and the small glands that control calcium. For most patients they are safe, short operations with a quick recovery, but the consequences that patients fear most, a changed voice, low calcium, and lifelong tablets, are specific to this area and must be discussed clearly. Thyroid surgery has also become more selective: many nodules need no operation, low-risk cancers may be watched or treated with a smaller operation, and the extent of surgery is now a shared decision. This chapter covers both glands.

**STEP 1 Explaining the conditions and the operations**

## 11.1 Why thyroid surgery is considered

Thyroid surgery is considered for four broad reasons (Figure 11.1). Nodules that are benign on fine-needle aspiration are usually observed and removed only if they are large, growing, or causing symptoms. Indeterminate nodules may be assessed with molecular testing where available, or by a diagnostic lobectomy. Thyroid cancer is treated according to its risk, and the 2025 American Thyroid Association guidelines place greater emphasis on active surveillance for small low-risk cancers and on lobectomy as the recommended operation for low-risk disease [1]. For a large goiter causing pressure symptoms, or for an overactive thyroid, surgery is one of several options; for Graves’ disease it is weighed against antithyroid drugs and radioactive iodine, and is often preferred by women planning pregnancy soon, by patients with a large goiter or significant eye disease, or by patient choice [2].

## 11.3 Low-risk thyroid cancer

For small, low-risk papillary thyroid cancers, patients now have real choices: active surveillance with regular ultrasound, lobectomy, and in selected cases ablation or total thyroidectomy [1]. Present these honestly, including the excellent outlook for most patients and the trade-offs of each option. After lobectomy, about 1 in 5 patients develop an underactive thyroid and need thyroxine [3]; a few need a second operation to remove the other side if the final pathology shows a higher-risk cancer.

## 11.4 Primary hyperparathyroidism

In primary hyperparathyroidism, usually one enlarged parathyroid gland (an adenoma) makes too much parathyroid hormone, raising blood calcium. Many patients have no obvious symptoms, but the condition can cause kidney stones, thinning bones, tiredness, low mood, and difficulty concentrating. Surgery is recommended for patients with symptoms, and for those without symptoms who meet criteria based on the calcium level, bone density, kidney function or stones, or age under 50 [4, 5]. Parathyroidectomy is the only cure; in experienced hands it cures about 95 or more in every 100 patients [5].

**STEP 2 Preparation, medicines, and recovery**

## 11.5 Before the operation

- **Voice assessment.** Ask about and record the voice before surgery, and examine the vocal cords (laryngoscopy) when the voice is abnormal, when there is a cancer that may involve the nerve, or after previous neck surgery [6, 7]. Ask about the patient’s voice needs: teachers, singers, imams, and others who rely on their voice need specific counseling.

<!-- -->

- **Thyroid function.** Patients with an overactive thyroid should be made euthyroid before surgery, as directed by the endocrinologist.

- **Calcium and vitamin D.** Check and correct vitamin D before total thyroidectomy and parathyroidectomy according to local protocol.

- **Medicines and fasting.** Use the standard plans in Chapters 2, 18, 22, and 23.

- **Pregnancy.** Discuss timing with women who are pregnant or planning pregnancy.

## 11.6 After the operation

Bleeding into the neck is rare but can compress the airway; it usually appears within the first hours after surgery. Nurses check the neck regularly, and the patient and family should know to call for help at once if the neck swells or breathing becomes difficult. After total thyroidectomy, calcium or parathyroid hormone is checked, and calcium supplements are given according to protocol; tingling of the lips, fingers, or toes, or muscle cramps, are the signs of low calcium. A hoarse, weak, or tired voice is common in the first weeks and usually improves; a voice change that persists should lead to examination of the vocal cords [6]. ## 11.7 Thyroxine and Ramadan

Thyroxine is best taken at the same time each day on an empty stomach, apart from calcium and iron supplements, which reduce its absorption. During Ramadan, agree a timing with the endocrinologist; common options are before suhoor or at bedtime, a few hours after iftar. Blood tests after Ramadan may show a small change that needs no action.

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

**“Will my voice change?”**

Many people have a hoarse or tired voice for a few days or weeks, which usually recovers. Permanent damage to a voice nerve is uncommon, about [X] in 100 in our unit. We check your voice before and after the operation.

**“Will I need tablets for life?”**

After total thyroidectomy, yes: one small thyroxine tablet a day. After removal of one side, about 1 in 5 people need thyroxine [3].

**“Will I gain weight?”**

Thyroxine replaces your hormone exactly when the dose is right. Weight gain is not caused by the operation itself; we check your blood levels and adjust the dose.

**“Can I have children afterward?”**

Yes. Thyroid surgery does not affect fertility. If you are on thyroxine, tell your doctor as soon as you are pregnant, because the dose often needs to increase.

**“What will the scar look like?”**

It is placed in a natural skin crease and usually fades to a thin line over months. Protect it from the sun for the first year.

**“Why only half the thyroid if I have cancer?”**

For small, low-risk cancers, removing one side gives the same excellent results as removing the whole gland, with fewer risks to the voice and calcium, and often no need for tablets [1].

**“Do I need radioactive iodine?”**

Not everyone does. It depends on the final pathology. Your endocrinologist will explain whether it is needed.

**“Can I fast in Ramadan on thyroxine?”**

Yes. Take it at the same time each day on an empty stomach, such as before suhoor or at bedtime. Agree the timing with your doctor.

**“Why did my lips tingle after the operation?”**

This is usually low calcium, because the calcium glands are temporarily stunned. Take the calcium we prescribed and tell us. It usually recovers within days or weeks.

**“Will my bones and kidneys get better after parathyroid surgery?”**

Bone strength usually improves after cure, and the risk of new kidney stones falls [4].
