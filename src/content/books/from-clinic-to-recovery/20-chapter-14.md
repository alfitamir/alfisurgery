# Chapter 14 — Colorectal Surgery and Living with a Stoma

Bowel surgery is performed for cancer, diverticular disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and emergencies such as obstruction or perforation. For many patients the operation itself is not what they fear most. It is the possibility of a stoma: an opening of the bowel on the abdomen, with a pouch to collect waste. A stoma touches body image, intimacy, cleanliness, religious practice, work, and how a person believes others see them. Most people adapt well with good preparation and support, but depressive symptoms affect almost half of patients after stoma surgery [1]. This chapter covers the counseling for colorectal surgery, with particular emphasis on the psychological care of the person with a stoma.

**STEP 1 Explaining the operation and the stoma**

## 14.1 Common reasons for bowel surgery

For bowel cancer, the operation is planned by the multidisciplinary team and may follow or precede chemotherapy or radiotherapy; breaking the news is covered in Section 1.7. For diverticulitis, the decision about elective resection is individualized according to the patient’s symptoms, complications, and quality of life, rather than the number of past episodes [2]. Inflammatory bowel disease and emergency operations raise the possibility of a stoma with little warning. All planned operations follow the enhanced recovery principles of Chapter 2, and preoperative education, including stoma education, shortens recovery [3].

## 14.2 Explaining a stoma

Explain what a stoma is, why it may be needed, which type is likely, and whether it is expected to be temporary or permanent (Figure 14.1). When a stoma is possible rather than certain, say so, and prepare the patient as though it will be made. Patients woken from an emergency operation with an unexpected stoma find adjustment much harder.

## 14.3 The psychological journey

Adjustment to a stoma follows a recognizable path (Figure 14.2). Before surgery, patients fear disfigurement, smell, and rejection. The first sight of the stoma can cause shock, disgust, or grief, and some patients refuse to look at it or touch it for days. In the first weeks at home, leaks, noise, and fear of odor lead some people to withdraw from family, work, and the mosque. Most adapt over months, but a substantial minority remain anxious, low, or isolated. In a meta-analysis, depressive symptoms were present in almost half of patients after stoma surgery, more often than in comparable patients without a stoma [1].

Several factors make adjustment harder: an unexpected or emergency stoma, a permanent stoma, a poorly sited stoma that leaks, lack of confidence in self-care, an unsupportive partner, and cultural or religious concern about cleanliness. Several things make it easier: preoperative counseling and site marking, early hands-on teaching, a peer who has lived with a stoma, and a partner or family member who is involved and accepting.

## 14.4 Supporting adjustment

- **Before surgery.** Every patient who may need a stoma should see a stoma nurse for counseling and marking of the stoma site, which improves outcomes and quality of life [4]. Offer a meeting with a trained volunteer who has a stoma.

<!-- -->

- **Family.** Ask the patient whom they want involved. A spouse who learns stoma care alongside the patient, and who is not shocked by it, is one of the strongest supports. Respect a patient who prefers to keep it private.

- **The first look.** Plan the first look with the nurse, at a time the patient chooses. Staff should keep calm, neutral expressions while handling the stoma; patients watch faces closely.

- **Self-care.** Aim for independence in emptying and changing the pouch before discharge. Confidence in self-care is closely linked to adjustment.

- **Practical fixes for fears.** Odor filters and deodorants, emptying before outings, pouch covers, high-waisted underwear, and a small kit for travel address the commonest worries.

- **Screening.** Ask about mood at every visit, using simple questions or a validated tool, and refer for psychological support when needed.

## 14.5 Faith and the stoma

For Muslim patients, the stoma raises immediate questions about ritual purity, prayer, fasting, and pilgrimage, and these concerns are a major reason for lower quality of life after stoma surgery [5]. Many patients are reluctant to ask, and many clinicians do not raise the subject. Reviews of Islamic scholarly opinion conclude that a person with a stoma can perform ablution and pray, attend the mosque, and perform Hajj and Umrah, and may fast when it is medically safe; the stoma is generally treated like other continuous conditions, so ablution is performed for each prayer time [5]. Religion-specific counseling before and after surgery improves quality of life [5].

## 14.6 Intimacy and relationships

Fear of rejection by a spouse is one of the deepest worries, and one that patients rarely raise. Ask gently, with the patient alone, and offer practical advice: emptying the pouch beforehand, using a small or opaque pouch or a cover, and choosing positions that avoid pressure on the stoma. Rectal surgery can affect the pelvic nerves, causing erectile or ejaculatory problems in men and pain or dryness in women; discuss this before surgery and offer referral if it occurs. Pregnancy is possible with a stoma, with specialist support.

**STEP 2 Preparation, medicines, and recovery**

## 14.7 Before the operation

- **Stoma counseling and site marking** by the stoma nurse, with the patient sitting, standing, and bending, and with their usual clothes and belt line in mind [4].

- **Bowel preparation and antibiotics** according to the unit protocol.

- **Enhanced recovery** as in Chapter 2: carbohydrate and fluid advice, early mobilization, and early feeding.

- **Medicines.** Blood thinners, diabetes medicines, and weight-loss injections need individual plans (Chapters 18, 22, and 23).

- **Supplies.** Make sure the patient knows how to obtain stoma supplies after discharge, including in other cities or when traveling.

## 14.8 After the operation

The stoma is swollen at first and shrinks over about six weeks, so the pouch size needs adjusting. An ileostomy produces liquid output, and high output can cause dehydration and kidney injury; teach patients to measure output, drink oral rehydration solution rather than only plain water, add salt, and seek help if output is high or they feel dizzy. Before discharge, the patient or a family member should be able to empty and change the pouch, and should know how to contact the stoma nurse.

## 14.9 Reversal and bowel function afterward

A temporary stoma is usually closed after the join has healed, often after a few months or after chemotherapy. Not every temporary stoma is reversed, because of complications, further treatment, or the patient’s health, and patients should know this [unit data]. After reversal of a stoma that protected a low rectal join, bowel function often changes, with urgency, frequency, clustering, or incontinence, known as low anterior resection syndrome. Major symptoms affect about 41 in 100 patients [6]. They often improve over the first one to two years, and treatments are available. Some patients with severe symptoms prefer to keep or return to a stoma.

**STEP 3 Questions patients ask**

**“Will people be able to smell it?”**

No. Modern pouches are sealed and have odor filters. You will notice a smell only when emptying or changing it, as with going to the toilet.

**“Will my husband or wife still find me attractive?”**

Most couples adjust well, especially when the partner is involved from the start. Many people find their partner is more accepting than they feared. If you would like, we can talk with both of you together.

**“Can I still pray?”**

Yes. Scholars agree that you can perform ablution and pray, and go to the mosque. Empty the pouch and clean around it before ablution, and perform ablution for each prayer time [5].

**“Can I fast in Ramadan or go for Umrah or Hajj?”**

Many people do. With a colostomy, fasting is usually possible once you have recovered. With an ileostomy, there is a risk of dehydration, so plan with us first. For pilgrimage, take plenty of supplies and plan for the heat [5].

**“Can I wear normal clothes, swim, and exercise?”**

Yes. Most people wear their usual clothes. Pouches are waterproof, and you can swim and exercise once recovered.

**“Will I be able to work?”**

Most people return to their usual work. If your work involves heavy lifting, we will discuss a support belt and a gradual return.

**“I cannot look at it. Is something wrong with me?”**

No. Many people feel this way at first. We will go at your pace, and most people find it gets easier within days or weeks.

**“Should I tell people about it?”**

That is your choice. Some people tell only close family; others are open. There is no right answer.

**“Will my stoma definitely be reversed?”**

We plan to reverse a temporary stoma, but not every one is closed, sometimes because of other treatment or health problems. We will review this with you.

**“What will my bowels be like after reversal?”**

After surgery low in the rectum, many people have frequent or urgent bowel movements at first. About 4 in 10 have major symptoms [6], which often improve over a year or two, and there are treatments.

**“Can I have children?”**

Yes. Pregnancy is possible with a stoma. Tell your team early so that we can support you.

**Your feelings are normal**

- Many people feel shocked, sad, angry, or embarrassed at first. This usually gets easier with time.

- Take your time to look at and touch your stoma. Your nurse will help you.

- Talk to someone you trust: your spouse, a family member, your nurse, or someone who has a stoma.

- If you feel low most days, lose interest in things, or avoid people, tell us. Help is available.

- **If you have thoughts of harming yourself, get help the same day.**

**1. Looking after your stoma**

- Empty the pouch when it is one-third to half full.

- Change it as your nurse showed you. Measure the stoma for the first weeks, because it shrinks.

- Clean the skin with warm water and dry it well. Do not use soap or cream unless your nurse advises.

- Order supplies before you run out. Supplier: \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

**2. Food and drink**

- Eat small, regular meals and chew well.

- **Ileostomy:** drink at least 1.5 to 2 liters a day, including oral rehydration drinks, and add a little salt to food. Measure your output if asked.

- Some foods cause gas or odor, such as onions, eggs, and fizzy drinks. Try foods one at a time.

**3. Prayer, fasting, and pilgrimage**

- You can perform ablution and pray, and go to the mosque.

- Empty the pouch and clean around it before ablution. Keep clean prayer clothes ready.

- Ask us before fasting, especially with an ileostomy.

- For Umrah or Hajj, take extra supplies in your hand luggage and drink plenty.

- You can talk to an imam or religious adviser if you wish.

**4. Clothes, intimacy, and daily life**

- Most people wear their usual clothes.

- Before intimacy, empty the pouch. A cover or smaller pouch may help you feel more confident.

- You can shower, swim, exercise, and travel once recovered.
