# Chapter 07 — Anal Cancer, Anal Intraepithelial Neoplasia, and Non-Healing Anorectal Lesions

*Purpose: any anorectal lesion that is indurated, ulcerated, bleeding, persistent, or recurrent is a possible cancer until a biopsy says otherwise — this chapter shows how to recognise it, whom to refer, and how fast.*

## 1. Anal and Perianal Malignancies at a Glance

Anal cancer is uncommon in the general population, but its incidence is much higher in defined risk groups, and it is often diagnosed late because early symptoms — bleeding, pain, itch, a lump — are attributed to haemorrhoids or fissure.

**Table 7.1 — Anal and perianal malignancies**

| Tumour | Who is at risk | Typical presentation | Key point for non-surgeons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anal squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) — the commonest anal cancer | HPV infection (mostly HPV 16), HIV, men who have sex with men, transplant recipients and other immunosuppression, prior cervical/vulvar/vaginal HSIL or cancer, smoking, receptive anal intercourse | Bleeding, pain, mass, pruritus, discharge, ulcer, faecal incontinence; inguinal lymphadenopathy | **Most are treated with chemoradiotherapy, not surgery** (ESMO 2021; ASCRS 2018). An unplanned “haemorrhoidectomy” of a cancer can complicate curative treatment — **biopsy first**. |
| Anal adenocarcinoma | Long-standing fistula, Crohn’s disease; rectal-type adenocarcinoma extending to the canal | Mass; mucinous discharge from a chronic fistula; induration around an old tract | Rare. Usually managed like rectal cancer; biopsy fistula tracts that change character. |
| Anorectal melanoma | Older adults; no clear behavioural risk factor | Bleeding; a polypoid or pigmented mass that may look like a thrombosed haemorrhoid; may be **amelanotic** | Rare and aggressive. A “thrombosed pile” that does not resolve, or a polypoid lesion, needs biopsy. |
| Perianal HSIL (formerly “Bowen disease”) and extramammary Paget disease | HPV (HSIL); older adults (Paget) | Persistent itchy, eczema-like, white, red, or pigmented plaque | Biopsy any plaque that persists 4–6 weeks despite treatment; Paget disease may signal an underlying adenocarcinoma (Chapter 15). |
| Others | HIV and immunosuppression (lymphoma, Kaposi sarcoma); verrucous carcinoma (giant condyloma) | Rapidly growing mass, ulcer, or large warty lesion | Specialist biopsy and multidisciplinary management. |

## 2. Anal Intraepithelial Neoplasia — the Modern Terminology

The Lower Anogenital Squamous Terminology (LAST) system uses two tiers for HPV-related precursor lesions. Older terms still appear in reports and referral letters.

**Table 7.2 — Squamous intraepithelial lesions**

| Current term | Older terms | Meaning | What happens next |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) | AIN 1, condyloma | Productive HPV infection; low malignant potential; often regresses | Treat symptomatic warts; surveillance per specialist |
| High-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) | AIN 2–3, carcinoma in situ, “Bowen disease” (perianal) | True cancer precursor | Specialist high-resolution anoscopy (HRA)-guided treatment and surveillance |
| Squamous cell carcinoma | — | Invasive cancer | Urgent staging and multidisciplinary team (MDT) |

> **ℹ️ EVIDENCE HIGHLIGHT**
>
> **Why this matters now [S]:** in the ANCHOR randomised trial (Palefsky et al. 2022) of people living with HIV with biopsy-proven anal HSIL, treating HSIL reduced progression to anal cancer by 57% compared with active monitoring. This result underpins current screening recommendations for high-risk groups.

## 3. Who Should Be Offered Anal Cancer Screening (IANS 2024) [C]

The International Anal Neoplasia Society set screening start ages using anal cancer incidence thresholds. Screening uses anal cytology, high-risk HPV testing (with HPV 16 genotyping), or co-testing, together with a digital anal–rectal examination (DARE). Abnormal results lead to HRA. **Availability of HRA varies widely between countries; follow local pathways [L].**

**Table 7.3 — Screening start points (IANS 2024)**

| Group | Start screening | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Men who have sex with men (MSM) and transgender women living with HIV | Age 35 | Highest-risk group |
| Other people living with HIV (women; men who have sex with women) | Age 45 | — |
| MSM and transgender women without HIV | Age 45 | — |
| Solid organ transplant recipients | 10 years after transplant | — |
| History of vulvar HSIL or vulvar cancer | Within 1 year of diagnosis | — |
| Others with HPV-related disease or immunosuppression (for example prior cervical or vaginal HSIL or cancer) | Shared decision-making, from about 45 | Lower absolute risk; consider individually |

<small>Where HRA is not available, many programmes rely on regular DARE to detect early cancers in high-risk groups [C][L]. Screening is for people **without** symptoms — any symptomatic patient follows the diagnostic pathway in section 5, not the screening pathway.</small>

## 4. Red-Flag Checklist for Non-Healing Perianal and Anal Lesions

**Any single item below means an urgent specialist referral and a biopsy — not another cream.**

| ☐ | Red flag |
|---|---|
| ☐ | Induration, a rolled or heaped edge, or a firm or fixed lesion |
| ☐ | Ulcer or “fissure” that is lateral, painless, or not healed after 4–6 weeks of appropriate treatment — or that keeps recurring |
| ☐ | A mass in the anal canal or at the margin, especially if friable or bleeding on contact |
| ☐ | Pigmented lesion, or a polypoid lesion that does not behave like a haemorrhoid |
| ☐ | A persistent plaque (white, red, or eczema-like) that does not respond within 4–6 weeks |
| ☐ | Palpable inguinal lymph nodes |
| ☐ | A chronic fistula with new pain, induration, a mass, bleeding, or mucinous discharge |
| ☐ | Warts that are ulcerated, indurated, bleeding, pigmented, or rapidly growing — or warts in a person with HIV or immunosuppression |
| ☐ | Any new anal symptom in a high-risk person (HIV, transplant, prior HPV-related cancer or HSIL) |
| ☐ | Weight loss, anaemia, or a change in bowel habit |

## 5. Why Persistent Lesions Must Not Be Labelled “Haemorrhoids”

- **Haemorrhoids are soft, compressible, and painless unless thrombosed; they do not ulcerate for weeks, become hard, or cause enlarged groin nodes.**

- **Treatments for “piles” can hide a cancer:** steroid and anaesthetic creams improve symptoms temporarily, while banding, cautery, or excision without histology can delay diagnosis or compromise curative chemoradiotherapy.

- **NICE NG12 (with DG56):** an unexplained anal mass or unexplained anal ulceration warrants consideration of a suspected-cancer pathway referral, and does **not** need a faecal immunochemical test (FIT) first [S].

## 6. Safe Examination, Biopsy, and the Referral Pathway

**Table 7.4 — From the first examination to the MDT**

| Step | What to do | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Examine | Inspection; palpation of the lesion (size in cm, fixity, induration); DRE (distance from the verge, sphincter involvement); anoscopy if tolerated; **both groins**; chaperone; clinical photograph with consent where policy allows. | ER, GP, specialist |
| Document | Anatomical quadrant, size, distance from the anal verge, appearance, nodes, and the red flags present. | Any clinician |
| Do not | Band, inject, cauterise, or excise a suspicious lesion without histology; prescribe steroid creams for an undiagnosed ulcer. | — |
| Biopsy | Perianal skin lesions: punch biopsy by a trained clinician (primary care, dermatology, or surgery). Anal canal lesions, painful lesions, and suspected cancer: specialist biopsy, often examination under anaesthesia. | Trained clinician / specialist |
| Test | **HIV test for every person with anal SCC or HSIL**; full blood count; renal function. Women: ensure cervical screening is up to date. | Referrer or specialist |
| Stage | MRI pelvis; CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or PET-CT (ESMO 2021). | Specialist |
| Treat | MDT plan. Anal SCC: chemoradiotherapy is standard for most; surgery mainly for small margin tumours or salvage. Adenocarcinoma and melanoma: specialist pathways. | Colorectal and oncology MDT |
| Follow up | Track the biopsy result and referral (“failsafe”); confirm the patient attended. | Referring clinician and nurse |

## 7. HPV Vaccination and Prevention

- **HPV vaccination [S]:** routine vaccination at age 11–12 (can start at 9), catch-up through age 26, and shared decision-making for adults aged 27–45 (ACIP 2019). WHO (2022) permits one- or two-dose schedules in young people, but recommends at least two, and ideally three, doses for people who are immunocompromised, including those living with HIV. National programmes differ — follow your national schedule [L].

- **Stop smoking:** smoking increases persistence of HPV infection and anal cancer risk.

- **HIV care:** effective antiretroviral therapy and regular review; screening as in Table 7.3.

- **Condoms** reduce, but do not eliminate, HPV transmission.

**Algorithm 7.1 — Non-healing or suspicious anorectal lesion**

```text
[ANAL OR PERIANAL LESION: ULCER, MASS, PLAQUE, OR "PILE"]
|
+-- Inspect + palpate + DRE + anoscopy + check BOTH groins
|
+-- ANY RED FLAG? induration, heaped edge, mass, contact
|   bleeding, pigment, lateral/painless ulcer, groin nodes,
|   chronic fistula with new mass or mucus, high-risk patient
|     -> YES: URGENT suspected-cancer referral (no FIT needed)
|        do NOT band, excise, or cauterise; offer HIV test
|        record size, site, distance from the verge
|        -> specialist biopsy / EUA -> MRI pelvis + CT
|           (or PET-CT) -> MDT
|
+-- No red flag and a benign cause is clear
|     -> treat it (fissure, haemorrhoid, dermatitis, wart)
|     -> REVIEW at 4-6 weeks
|        +-- healed -> discharge with safety-net advice
|        +-- not healed, recurred, or changing
|              -> treat as a RED FLAG: urgent referral
|
+-- High-risk person with NO symptoms (HIV, transplant,
    prior vulvar HSIL/cancer, MSM or TW)
      -> offer screening per IANS 2024 where available
         (cytology and/or hrHPV + DARE) -> HRA if abnormal
```

## 8. Dangerous Mimics

**Table 7.5 — Benign-looking lesions that may be malignant**

| It looks like… | It may be… | Clue |
|---|---|---|
| A thrombosed haemorrhoid | Anorectal melanoma | Persists beyond 2–3 weeks; polypoid; pigment may be absent |
| A chronic fissure | Anal SCC | Off-midline, indurated, heaped edges, not healing after 6–8 weeks |
| A perianal abscess or fistula | SCC or adenocarcinoma | Hard mass, mucinous discharge, long-standing tract that changes |
| Anal warts | HSIL, verrucous carcinoma, or SCC | Ulceration, induration, bleeding, rapid growth, immunosuppression |
| Pruritus ani or eczema | Perianal HSIL or Paget disease | Well-demarcated plaque that does not respond to treatment |
| A Crohn’s ulcer | Carcinoma (both can coexist) | New mass, new pain, or change in long-standing perianal Crohn’s disease |
| A syphilitic chancre | SCC (or vice versa) | Indurated ulcer with groin nodes — test for syphilis **and** biopsy if it does not heal |

## 9. Role-Targeted Action Boxes

> **🚑 ER DOCTOR ACTION BOX**
>
> - **Do not discharge an ulcerated or indurated anal mass as a “thrombosed pile”.** Document size, site, and groin nodes; refer urgently with a named follow-up route.
> - **Tumour emergencies:** heavy bleeding (resuscitate, direct pressure, surgical review), obstruction, perforation, or an abscess around a tumour — same-day surgical review; send drained tissue for histology.
> - **Offer an HIV test** when anal cancer or HSIL is suspected.

> **🩺 FAMILY MEDICINE / GP CLINIC BOX**
>
> - **Apply the 4–6-week rule:** any anal lesion treated as benign must be re-examined; if it has not healed, refer urgently for biopsy.
> - **Refer an unexplained anal mass or ulcer on the suspected-cancer pathway** — no FIT needed (NICE NG12/DG56).
> - **Ask about previous cervical, vulvar, or vaginal HPV disease, HIV, transplant, and smoking.** Check cervical screening and HPV vaccination status.

> **🏥 ER & WARD NURSE CARE AND SBAR BOX**
>
> - **Safety-net and track:** give the patient a written review date; confirm urgent referrals and biopsy results are received and acted on.
> - **Communicate with care:** offer a chaperone, explain each step, and signpost psychological and cancer-nurse support once a diagnosis is made.

> **💊 CROSS-SPECIALTY PRESCRIBING TRAP**
>
> - **Repeat prescriptions of haemorrhoid creams without re-examination** can mask cancer for months.
> - **Immunosuppressants** (transplant, biologics) increase the risk of persistent HPV disease; such patients need a lower threshold for examination and referral.

> **⛔ NEVER DO THIS**
>
> - Never band, cauterise, or excise a suspicious anal lesion without histology.
> - Never call an ulcer that has not healed after 6–8 weeks a “chronic fissure” without specialist review.
> - Never forget the groins — inguinal nodes change staging and urgency.
> - Never omit an HIV test in a patient with anal SCC or HSIL.

> **📝 COMMON DOCUMENTATION ERROR**
>
> - **“Haemorrhoids”** recorded for a firm 3 cm lesion, with no size, site, distance from the verge, or nodal examination.
> - **No safety-net date** and no record of who will chase the biopsy result.

**📨 REFERRAL URGENCY BOX**

| Urgency | Clinical trigger | Who and how fast |
|---|---|---|
| 🔴 EMERGENCY | Anorectal tumour with heavy bleeding and instability, obstruction, or perforation | On-call surgeon now; resuscitate |
| 🔴 SAME-DAY | Tumour with surrounding abscess or sepsis | Surgical drainage with biopsy |
| 🟡 URGENT | Suspected anal cancer (mass, ulcer, induration, pigment, groin nodes); non-healing lesion after 4–6 weeks; suspected HSIL in a high-risk person | Suspected-cancer pathway (typically within 2 weeks); colorectal surgery; HRA service |
| 🟢 ROUTINE | Asymptomatic high-risk person eligible for screening; uncomplicated warts in an immunocompetent person | Screening programme or sexual-health clinic |

## The Patient in Front of You — Chapter 07 Mini-Case

> **🧑‍⚕️ THE PATIENT IN FRONT OF YOU**
>
> A 52-year-old woman has had “piles” for 6 months, treated with three different creams. She now has bleeding and pain on sitting. She was treated for cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 3 ten years ago and smokes 15 cigarettes a day. On examination there is a 2 cm indurated ulcer with heaped edges at the left lateral anal margin, and a firm 1.5 cm left inguinal lymph node.

**Step 1** **Recognise the red flags:** induration, heaped edge, lateral ulcer, groin node, and a history of HPV-related cervical disease.

**Step 2** **Do not treat as haemorrhoids:** stop the creams; no banding or excision.

**Step 3** **Refer urgently** on the suspected-cancer pathway with size, site, distance from the verge, and nodal findings documented; offer an HIV test; check cervical screening.

**Step 4** **Expected pathway:** specialist biopsy confirms SCC; MRI pelvis and CT or PET-CT stage the disease; the MDT plans chemoradiotherapy; smoking-cessation support begins now.

## Board-Style Question — Chapter 07

**A 41-year-old man who has sex with men and has lived with HIV for 12 years (undetectable viral load on antiretroviral therapy) asks about anal cancer prevention. He has no anal symptoms and a normal examination. What is the most appropriate advice?**

- **A.** No screening is needed unless symptoms develop
- **B.** Offer anal cancer screening now (anal cytology and/or high-risk HPV testing with a digital anal–rectal examination), with high-resolution anoscopy if abnormal
- **C.** Colonoscopy every 5 years from now
- **D.** Annual serum CEA measurement

**Answer and explanation**

- ❌ **A** Waiting for symptoms misses the window to treat HSIL before it becomes cancer.
- ✅ **B** **Correct.** IANS 2024 recommends starting screening at age 35 for MSM and transgender women living with HIV. The ANCHOR trial showed that treating HSIL reduces progression to anal cancer by 57%.
- ❌ **C** Colonoscopy screens for colorectal neoplasia and does not assess the anal transformation zone adequately for HSIL.
- ❌ **D** CEA is not a screening test for anal cancer.

## Five Most Dangerous Mistakes — Chapter 07

1. ⚠️ Repeating haemorrhoid creams for months for an indurated ulcer.
2. ⚠️ Excising a “pile” that is actually an anal cancer, without histology.
3. ⚠️ Missing inguinal nodes because the groins were not examined.
4. ⚠️ Treating a non-resolving “thrombosed pile” conservatively and missing melanoma.
5. ⚠️ Failing to track an urgent referral or biopsy result.
