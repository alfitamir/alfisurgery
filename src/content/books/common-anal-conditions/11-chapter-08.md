# Chapter 08 — Rectal Bleeding and Colorectal Cancer Triage

_Purpose: visible rectal bleeding is a symptom, not a diagnosis — decide in minutes whether it is dangerous now, then decide whether the colon must be examined before anyone treats haemorrhoids._

## 1. Differential Diagnosis by Age and Context

**Table 8.1 — Common causes and must-not-miss diagnoses**

| Context                                                  | Most likely                                                                | Must not miss                                                                                                                                              |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Young adult, bright-red blood on the paper               | Fissure (painful) or haemorrhoids (painless)                               | IBD (diarrhoea, urgency, nocturnal symptoms), infectious colitis, STI proctitis, **early-onset colorectal cancer** (incidence is rising in younger adults) |
| Older adult (around 45–50 and above)                     | Haemorrhoids, diverticular bleeding (painless, often large volume), polyps | **Colorectal cancer**, angiodysplasia, ischaemic colitis (sudden crampy left-sided pain then bloody diarrhoea in a patient with vascular risk factors)     |
| Haemodynamically unstable patient with “rectal bleeding” | Diverticular bleeding                                                      | **Upper GI bleeding** with rapid transit (melaena, raised urea, haematemesis, prior ulcer or varices)                                                      |
| After polypectomy (up to about 2 weeks)                  | Post-polypectomy bleeding                                                  | Perforation if pain or fever                                                                                                                               |
| Patient on anticoagulants or antiplatelets               | Any of the above                                                           | Antithrombotics **unmask** lesions — they are not the diagnosis; still investigate                                                                         |
| Previous pelvic radiotherapy                             | Radiation proctitis (Chapter 15)                                           | Recurrent or new cancer                                                                                                                                    |
| Cirrhosis                                                | Haemorrhoids                                                               | Rectal varices                                                                                                                                             |
| Children                                                 | Fissure, juvenile polyp (Chapter 14)                                       | Intussusception, Meckel’s diverticulum, IBD                                                                                                                |

## 2. What the Blood Tells You — and What It Cannot

**Table 8.2 — Characterising the bleeding**

| Pattern                                                      | Points towards                                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Bright red on the paper or dripping into the pan after stool | Anal outlet source (haemorrhoids, fissure) — **but does not exclude a rectal cancer**      |
| Dark red blood mixed with stool                              | More proximal source: colorectal cancer, IBD, diverticular disease                         |
| Black, tarry stool (melaena)                                 | Upper GI or right-colon source (note: oral iron blackens stool but is not sticky or tarry) |
| Large painless volume with clots                             | Diverticular bleeding or angiodysplasia                                                    |
| Bloody diarrhoea with fever or abdominal pain                | Infectious colitis, IBD, ischaemic colitis                                                 |
| Blood with mucus and tenesmus                                | Proctitis (IBD, STI, radiation) or rectal cancer                                           |

<small>The colour and timing of blood are useful clues, but no pattern reliably excludes cancer [C]. Decisions rest on alarm features, examination, and — where available — FIT.</small>

## 3. Alarm Features — Examine the Colon Before Blaming Haemorrhoids

**Table 8.3 — Alarm features**

| Alarm feature                                                                                                                                                 | Why it matters                                                                                                                                            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| At or above the national screening start age without up-to-date screening (45 in the United States; 50 in many other countries)                               | Colorectal neoplasia risk rises with age (USPSTF 2021)                                                                                                    |
| **Iron-deficiency anaemia** in any man, any postmenopausal woman, or unexplained in a premenopausal woman                                                     | About a third of men and postmenopausal women with IDA have an underlying lesion, mostly in the GI tract — bidirectional endoscopy is standard (BSG 2021) |
| Unexplained weight loss                                                                                                                                       | Constitutional sign of malignancy or IBD                                                                                                                  |
| Persistent change in bowel habit (looser, more frequent, or narrower stools; nocturnal symptoms; tenesmus)                                                    | Classic feature of colorectal cancer or IBD                                                                                                               |
| Family history: first-degree relative with colorectal cancer (especially under about 60) or two or more affected relatives; known Lynch syndrome or polyposis | Lower threshold and earlier colonoscopy                                                                                                                   |
| Personal history of IBD, colorectal cancer, or advanced adenoma                                                                                               | Surveillance needs                                                                                                                                        |
| Abdominal or rectal mass                                                                                                                                      | Direct suspected-cancer referral (no FIT needed for a rectal mass)                                                                                        |
| Positive FIT (≥ 10 µg haemoglobin per gram of faeces in symptomatic pathways)                                                                                 | Suspected-cancer pathway referral (NICE DG56)                                                                                                             |
| Bleeding persisting or recurring after 4–6 weeks of appropriate haemorrhoid or fissure treatment                                                              | The source may not be the anus                                                                                                                            |

> **⚠️ EARLY-ONSET COLORECTAL CANCER**
>
> **Young age does not exclude colorectal cancer.** Persistent bleeding, a change in bowel habit, or anaemia in a 30- or 40-year-old still needs investigation.

**Algorithm 8.1 — Visible rectal bleeding in the ER or clinic**

```text
[VISIBLE RECTAL BLEEDING]
|
STEP 1 - IS IT DANGEROUS NOW?
|  HR, BP, shock index (HR / systolic BP), postural drop,
|  syncope, ongoing heavy bleeding, Hb, antithrombotics
|
+-- Shock index > 1 after resuscitation, or ongoing heavy
|   bleeding -> RED: resuscitate; senior + surgery/GI;
|   group and crossmatch; CT angiography to localise
|   (BSG 2019); melaena or raised urea -> think UPPER GI
|
+-- Stable -> STEP 2
|
STEP 2 - WHERE IS IT COMING FROM?
|  Inspect, DRE, anoscopy: fissure? haemorrhoids? mass?
|
+-- Rectal or anal MASS or ulcer
|     -> YELLOW: urgent suspected-cancer referral now
|        (no FIT needed; Chapter 07 for anal lesions)
|
STEP 3 - MUST THE COLON BE EXAMINED FIRST?
|  Any alarm feature? (age/screening, IDA, weight loss,
|  bowel-habit change, family history, IBD, positive FIT,
|  persistent bleeding)
|
+-- YES -> YELLOW: colonoscopy (or CT colonography if
|     colonoscopy unsuitable) BEFORE haemorrhoid procedures
|
+-- NO, and a clear anal source is seen
      -> GREEN: treat fissure/haemorrhoids (Chapters 03-04)
      -> REVIEW at 4-6 weeks: still bleeding?
         -> treat as an alarm feature: colon evaluation
```

## 4. When Haemorrhoid Treatment May Proceed — and When the Colon Comes First

**Table 8.4 — Treat now or investigate first**

| 🟢 Haemorrhoid or fissure treatment may proceed                                                                          | 🟡 Colon evaluation must come first                                                                         |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Below the screening age, no alarm features, typical outlet bleeding, and a clear anal source on examination and anoscopy | Any alarm feature in Table 8.3                                                                              |
| Up-to-date high-quality colonoscopy (within the surveillance interval) and no new features                               | Bleeding that is dark, mixed with stool, or accompanied by mucus or tenesmus                                |
| Bleeding that stops with treatment and stays stopped                                                                     | Bleeding that persists or recurs after 4–6 weeks of treatment                                               |
| Conservative care (fibre, toilet habits, topical therapy) while awaiting investigation                                   | Before **any** banding, haemorrhoidectomy, or other procedure in a patient with alarm features (ASCRS 2024) |

## 5. Investigations and Referral Pathways

**Table 8.5 — Choosing the test**

| Test                                                                                        | Use                                                                                                                         | Caveats                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Full blood count, ferritin/iron studies, urea and creatinine, coagulation, group and screen | Every significant bleed; IDA work-up                                                                                        | Raised urea out of proportion to creatinine suggests an upper GI source                                                                                                                          |
| Quantitative FIT                                                                            | Triage of symptomatic adults in primary care where the pathway exists: ≥ 10 µg Hb/g → suspected-cancer referral (NICE DG56) | Not needed for a rectal or anal mass or anal ulcer; **a negative FIT does not override strong clinical concern** — refer anyway. FIT detects human haemoglobin and is not affected by oral iron. |
| Colonoscopy                                                                                 | Reference standard: visualises, biopsies, and removes polyps                                                                | Bowel preparation; caution with sodium phosphate preparations in renal impairment                                                                                                                |
| Flexible sigmoidoscopy                                                                      | Selected younger patients with outlet-type bleeding and no alarm features, per local pathway [L]                            | Does not examine the proximal colon                                                                                                                                                              |
| CT colonography                                                                             | Frail patients, incomplete colonoscopy, or patient preference                                                               | No biopsy; positive findings need endoscopy                                                                                                                                                      |
| CT angiography                                                                              | Acute severe bleeding or shock index > 1 after resuscitation (BSG 2019)                                                     | Needs active bleeding to localise; positive scan → interventional radiology or endoscopy                                                                                                         |
| Upper GI endoscopy                                                                          | Suspected upper source; IDA (as part of bidirectional endoscopy)                                                            | —                                                                                                                                                                                                |

## 6. Acute Severe Bleeding — Key ER Numbers (BSG 2019)

- **Shock index (heart rate ÷ systolic BP) > 1 after initial resuscitation = unstable:** CT angiography to localise, then interventional radiology or endoscopy; consider an upper GI source if no lower source is found [S/C].

- **Stable patients:** the Oakland score helps decide disposal — a score of 8 or less identifies patients who can usually be discharged for outpatient investigation [C]. The score was derived in the UK; apply with clinical judgement.

- **Transfusion:** restrictive approach — threshold about 70 g/L with a target of 70–90 g/L; about 80 g/L with a target of 100 g/L in patients with cardiovascular disease [C].

- **Antithrombotics [C][L]:** do not routinely stop aspirin taken for secondary prevention; withhold warfarin or DOACs in major bleeding and reverse for life-threatening haemorrhage per local protocol; involve cardiology or haematology for recent stents or high thrombotic risk.

## 7. Documentation Template for Rectal Bleeding

**Table 8.6 — Rectal bleeding documentation template**

| Field                        | Record                                                                                                         |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Onset and duration           | Date of first episode; continuous or intermittent                                                              |
| Frequency and volume         | Episodes per week; spots, streaks, dripping, clots, or large volume                                            |
| Colour and relation to stool | Bright red / dark red / melaena; on paper, in pan, or mixed with stool                                         |
| Associated symptoms          | Pain on defecation, prolapse, mucus, tenesmus, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, nocturnal symptoms                   |
| Alarm features               | Change in bowel habit, weight loss, family history, IBD, previous colonoscopy (date and result)                |
| Medications                  | Antiplatelets, anticoagulants (agent, dose, last dose), NSAIDs, iron                                           |
| Observations                 | HR, BP, **shock index**, postural change, temperature, NEWS2                                                   |
| Examination                  | Abdomen; inspection; DRE (mass, blood, stool); anoscopy findings by anatomical quadrant; groins if anal lesion |
| Results                      | Hb, MCV, ferritin, urea, coagulation, FIT (value and date)                                                     |
| Assessment                   | Most likely source **and** what has been excluded                                                              |
| Plan                         | Treatment, investigation, referral (pathway and urgency)                                                       |
| Safety-net                   | Written return triggers and a dated review (4–6 weeks)                                                         |

> **💬 PATIENT EXPLANATION — WHY “IT LOOKS LIKE PILES” IS NOT A DIAGNOSIS**
>
> _“I can see some swollen veins (piles) that could explain your bleeding. But piles are very common, and they can sit next to something more important higher up in the bowel that I cannot see from here. Because of [your age / your blood test / the change in your bowel habit / your family history], the safe step is to look at the whole bowel first. Most people turn out to have a simple cause — but if there is something else, finding it early makes it much easier to treat.”_
> **Return straight away if:** you pass a lot of blood or clots, feel faint or dizzy, or have severe tummy pain.

## 8. Dangerous Mimics

**Table 8.7 — Rectal bleeding mimics**

| Presumed cause                         | Actual cause                            | Clue                                                                                    |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Haemorrhoids                           | Rectal or colon cancer                  | Alarm features; mass on DRE; bleeding persists despite treatment                        |
| Diverticular bleed                     | Upper GI bleed                          | Melaena, haematemesis, raised urea, haemodynamic instability                            |
| Gastroenteritis                        | Ischaemic colitis or IBD                | Vascular risk factors and sudden pain (ischaemia); chronic diarrhoea, weight loss (IBD) |
| “Piles” in cirrhosis                   | Rectal varices                          | Portal hypertension; bluish channels above the dentate line                             |
| Haemorrhoids after pelvic radiotherapy | Radiation proctitis or recurrent cancer | Telangiectasia on endoscopy; history of radiotherapy                                    |
| Anticoagulant “side effect”            | Underlying neoplasm                     | Bleeding on therapeutic anticoagulation still needs investigation                       |

## 9. Role-Targeted Action Boxes

> **🚑 ER DOCTOR ACTION BOX**
>
> - **Calculate the shock index for every rectal bleed.** Above 1 after resuscitation: senior review, crossmatch, CT angiography, and the surgical or GI team.
> - **Melaena or a raised urea in an unstable patient** suggests an upper GI source — arrange upper endoscopy.
> - **A stable patient with a low Oakland score** can usually be discharged with an outpatient investigation plan — not with a cream and no follow-up.

> **🩺 FAMILY MEDICINE / GP CLINIC BOX**
>
> - **Examine every patient with rectal bleeding** (inspection, DRE, anoscopy where available) and check Hb and ferritin.
> - **Screen for alarm features** and use FIT where your pathway provides it; refer directly for a rectal or anal mass or anal ulcer.
> - **Book a 4–6-week review** for patients treated for an anal source; persistent bleeding becomes an alarm feature.

> **🏥 ER & WARD NURSE CARE AND SBAR BOX**
>
> - **SBAR escalation triggers [C][L]:** shock index > 1; systolic BP < 90 mmHg; heart rate > 120/min; repeated passage of fresh blood or clots; new confusion or syncope; or a haemoglobin fall of 20 g/L or more.
> - **Record a stool and bleeding chart,** and keep large-bore IV access patent in admitted patients.

> **💊 CROSS-SPECIALTY PRESCRIBING TRAP**
>
> - **Iron without investigation:** treating IDA with oral iron and no endoscopic work-up can hide a colorectal cancer. Investigate and replace iron together.
> - **Oral iron darkens stool** and can be mistaken for melaena (it is not sticky or tarry); it does not cause a positive FIT.

> **⛔ NEVER DO THIS**
>
> - Never attribute iron-deficiency anaemia to haemorrhoids before the GI tract has been investigated.
> - Never refer for banding or haemorrhoidectomy without colon evaluation when alarm features are present.
> - Never discharge a patient whose shock index stays above 1.
> - Never let a negative FIT override strong clinical suspicion of cancer.
> - Never assume a young patient cannot have colorectal cancer.

> **📝 COMMON DOCUMENTATION ERROR**
>
> - **“PR bleed — likely piles”** without observations, examination, haemoglobin, alarm-feature screen, or follow-up plan.
> - **A FIT result filed without action,** or a positive FIT without a referral date.

**📨 REFERRAL URGENCY BOX**

| Urgency      | Clinical trigger                                                                                            | Who and how fast                                                           |
| ------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 🔴 EMERGENCY | Shock index > 1 after resuscitation, ongoing heavy bleeding, or haemodynamic instability                    | Resuscitate; senior, surgical/GI, interventional radiology; CT angiography |
| 🔴 SAME-DAY  | Stable but major bleed (high Oakland score), symptomatic anaemia, or significant bleeding on anticoagulants | Admission and inpatient investigation                                      |
| 🟡 URGENT    | Alarm features; positive FIT; rectal or abdominal mass; anal mass or ulcer                                  | Suspected-cancer pathway (typically within 2 weeks)                        |
| 🟢 ROUTINE   | Younger patient, no alarm features, clear anal source responding to treatment                               | Treat and review at 4–6 weeks; national screening when eligible            |

## The Patient in Front of You — Chapter 08 Mini-Case

> **🧑‍⚕️ THE PATIENT IN FRONT OF YOU**
>
> A 47-year-old man is referred by his GP for “banding of bleeding piles”. He has had intermittent bright-red bleeding for 3 months and feels more tired than usual. Haemoglobin is 112 g/L, MCV 74 fL, and ferritin 9 µg/L. His father had colon cancer at 58. Anoscopy shows grade II internal haemorrhoids.

**Step 1** **Count the alarm features:** iron-deficiency anaemia, a first-degree relative with colorectal cancer, and age at or near screening age.

**Step 2** **Do not band yet.** Haemorrhoids on anoscopy do not explain iron-deficiency anaemia.

**Step 3** **Investigate:** urgent bidirectional endoscopy (colonoscopy and upper endoscopy) per BSG 2021, with iron replacement started alongside.

**Step 4** **Outcome and lesson:** colonoscopy shows a 3 cm sigmoid adenocarcinoma; the haemorrhoids were bystanders. Treat the haemorrhoids only after the colon has been cleared or the cancer managed.

## Board-Style Question — Chapter 08

**A 74-year-old woman taking apixaban for atrial fibrillation presents with large-volume maroon rectal bleeding. After 1 litre of crystalloid her heart rate is 118/min and systolic BP 92 mmHg (shock index 1.28). Upper GI symptoms are absent and urea is normal. What is the most appropriate next step?**

- **A.** Discharge with an outpatient colonoscopy in 2 weeks
- **B.** CT angiography after resuscitation, with senior, surgical/GI, and interventional radiology involvement
- **C.** Rubber band ligation of visible internal haemorrhoids
- **D.** Faecal immunochemical test and review in clinic

**Answer and explanation**

- ❌ **A** An unstable patient with ongoing bleeding cannot be discharged.
- ✅ **B** **Correct.** A shock index above 1 after resuscitation defines an unstable lower GI bleed; BSG 2019 recommends CT angiography to localise bleeding before radiological or endoscopic therapy. Withhold apixaban and use reversal per local protocol if bleeding is life-threatening; consider an upper GI source if CT angiography is negative.
- ❌ **C** Banding does not address a large-volume maroon bleed, which is unlikely to be haemorrhoidal.
- ❌ **D** FIT is a triage tool for stable symptomatic patients in primary care, not for acute haemorrhage.

## Five Most Dangerous Mistakes — Chapter 08

1. ⚠️ Banding haemorrhoids in a patient with iron-deficiency anaemia and missing a colorectal cancer.
2. ⚠️ Discharging an unstable bleeding patient because “it is only piles”.
3. ⚠️ Missing an upper GI bleed presenting as maroon rectal bleeding.
4. ⚠️ Accepting a negative FIT despite a mass, weight loss, or persistent symptoms.
5. ⚠️ Dismissing persistent bleeding in a young adult.
