# Chapter 14 — Paediatric and Adolescent Anorectal Conditions

*Purpose: children are not small adults — most anorectal problems in childhood are functional or self-limiting, but intussusception, streptococcal dermatitis, cystic fibrosis, Hirschsprung disease, and abuse must never be missed.*

## 1. Examining a Child

- **Explain to the child and the parent;** a parent or guardian and a chaperone should be present.

- **Inspection is usually enough.** A DRE is not needed to diagnose functional constipation and should be reserved for diagnostic uncertainty or red flags, performed by an experienced clinician (ESPGHAN/NASPGHAN 2014; NICE CG99) [S].

- **Never force an examination;** consider examination under anaesthesia when it is essential.

- **Adolescents:** offer part of the consultation alone; explain confidentiality and its limits; consent rules vary by country [L].

## 2. Common and Must-Not-Miss Conditions

**Table 14.1 — Paediatric anorectal conditions**

| Condition | Typical presentation | Management | Red flags |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional constipation and impaction | Withholding, large or hard painful stools, soiling (overflow) | Macrogol for disimpaction then maintenance; toilet routine; diet and fluids; continue for months (ESPGHAN/NASPGHAN 2014) [S] | Delayed meconium (> 48 h), onset in the first month, poor growth, ribbon stools, abnormal lower-limb neurology, sacral dimple or tuft, bilious vomiting → paediatric referral (Hirschsprung, spinal causes) |
| Anal fissure | Pain and bright-red streaks with hard stools | Treat constipation; warm baths; barrier cream | Lateral or multiple fissures, diarrhoea, poor growth → IBD; unexplained injury → safeguarding |
| Threadworm (pinworm) | Night-time anal itch, disturbed sleep | Tape test if unsure; mebendazole for the child and household, repeated after 2 weeks, with hygiene; under-2s per formulary [L] | — |
| Perianal streptococcal dermatitis | Sharply demarcated bright-red perianal rash, pain on defecation, blood streaks; often misdiagnosed as candida or nappy rash | Swab; oral penicillin V or amoxicillin (about 10 days) [C] | Recurrence; guttate psoriasis may follow |
| Juvenile polyp | Painless bright-red bleeding, usually age 2–10; may prolapse | Paediatric colonoscopy and polypectomy | Multiple polyps → polyposis syndrome assessment |
| Rectal prolapse | Toddlers; red mass after straining or diarrhoea | Gentle reduction; treat constipation or diarrhoea; usually resolves | Recurrent → **sweat test for cystic fibrosis**; malnutrition, parasites |
| Perianal abscess and fistula | Infants (often boys under 1 year); older children | Infants: drainage; many fistulas resolve. Older children: drainage and look for Crohn’s disease | Recurrent or complex disease → IBD work-up |
| Intussusception | Age about 6 months–3 years; colicky pain, pallor, lethargy, vomiting, “redcurrant jelly” stool | 🔴 Emergency ultrasound and paediatric surgery | Shock, peritonism |
| Haemolytic uraemic syndrome | Bloody diarrhoea then pallor, reduced urine, bruising | 🔴 Urgent bloods and paediatric team; avoid antimotility drugs | Oliguria, anaemia, thrombocytopenia |

## 3. Sexual Abuse Red Flags and Safeguarding

**Every clinician has a duty to act on safeguarding concerns according to national law [L].** Recognise; do not investigate.

**Table 14.2 — Safeguarding indicators and actions**

| Raise concern | Interpret with caution | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Disclosure by the child | Perianal redness, fissures, or soreness (common with constipation and hygiene problems) | Ensure the child is safe now |
| Gonorrhoea, chlamydia, syphilis, or trichomonas beyond the neonatal period | Reflex anal dilatation alone | Record the child’s words verbatim; do not repeat interviews or lead the child |
| Unexplained anogenital injury, bruising, or bleeding | Anogenital warts in a young child (may be non-sexually acquired — still needs specialist assessment) | Follow the local child-protection procedure immediately |
| Pregnancy in a young adolescent; sexualised behaviour inappropriate for age | — | Refer for specialist paediatric forensic examination; do **not** perform a detailed forensic examination yourself |
| Inconsistent history, delayed presentation, or other injuries | — | STI testing and HIV PEP may still be needed — via the specialist team |

<small>Reference: RCPCH, The Physical Signs of Child Sexual Abuse (2015) [C].</small>

## 4. Dangerous Mimics

**Table 14.3 — Paediatric mimics**

| Looks like | Could be | Clue |
|---|---|---|
| Nappy rash or candida | Perianal streptococcal dermatitis | Sharply demarcated, painful, no satellite lesions |
| Constipation | Hirschsprung disease | Delayed meconium, distension, poor growth |
| Gastroenteritis with blood | Intussusception or HUS | Lethargy, colicky pain; pallor, low urine |
| “Piles” in a child | Juvenile polyp or prolapse | Painless bleeding; concentric folds |
| Constipation-related fissure | Abuse-related injury, or Crohn’s disease | Unexplained or atypical injury; lateral fissures and poor growth |

## 5. Role-Targeted Action Boxes

> **🚑 ER DOCTOR ACTION BOX**
>
> - **Redcurrant-jelly stool, colicky pain, and lethargy** = intussusception until proven otherwise — urgent ultrasound and paediatric surgery.
> - **Avoid antimotility drugs in children with bloody diarrhoea** (HUS risk).
> - **Safeguarding concerns are acted on in the ER,** not deferred to “follow-up”.

> **🩺 FAMILY MEDICINE / GP CLINIC BOX**
>
> - **Treat constipation adequately and for long enough** — under-treatment is the commonest cause of failure.
> - **Swab a bright-red perianal rash** before calling it candida.
> - **Recurrent rectal prolapse → sweat test.**

> **🏥 ER & WARD NURSE CARE AND SBAR BOX**
>
> - **Child-friendly approach:** distraction, parent present, chaperone, explanation at the child’s level.
> - **Escalate safeguarding concerns** to the named safeguarding lead the same day.

> **💊 CROSS-SPECIALTY PRESCRIBING TRAP**
>
> - **Adult doses and adult drugs:** check paediatric formulary doses by weight; avoid stimulant laxatives as the sole long-term therapy without specialist advice; mebendazole age restrictions.
> - **Topical steroids and GTN** are not routine in children.

> **⛔ NEVER DO THIS**
>
> - Never force an anorectal examination on a child.
> - Never perform a detailed forensic examination yourself when abuse is suspected.
> - Never treat a bright-red perianal rash as candida without considering streptococcus.
> - Never give antimotility drugs to a child with bloody diarrhoea.

> **📝 COMMON DOCUMENTATION ERROR**
>
> - **Safeguarding notes** without the child’s exact words, who was present, and the actions taken.
> - **Constipation notes** without growth, meconium history, and neurological examination.

**📨 REFERRAL URGENCY BOX**

| Urgency | Clinical trigger | Who and how fast |
|---|---|---|
| 🔴 EMERGENCY | Suspected intussusception, volvulus, HUS, Hirschsprung enterocolitis, or shock | Paediatric emergency and surgery now |
| 🔴 SAME-DAY | Safeguarding concern; perianal abscess in an infant or child | Safeguarding lead and child-protection team; paediatric surgery |
| 🟡 URGENT | Painless rectal bleeding (polyp); recurrent prolapse; constipation with red flags; suspected IBD | Paediatric gastroenterology or surgery |
| 🟢 ROUTINE | Functional constipation not responding to adequate treatment | Paediatric clinic |

## The Patient in Front of You — Chapter 14 Mini-Case

> **🧑‍⚕️ THE PATIENT IN FRONT OF YOU**
>
> A 5-year-old boy has had 3 weeks of painful defecation, blood streaks on the paper, and a red perianal rash treated as nappy rash with an antifungal cream. The rash is bright red, sharply demarcated, and tender, with no satellite lesions. His sister recently had a sore throat.

**Step 1** **Rethink the rash:** sharply demarcated, painful, no satellite lesions, sibling with sore throat → perianal streptococcal dermatitis.

**Step 2** **Confirm:** perianal swab for group A streptococcus.

**Step 3** **Treat:** oral penicillin V or amoxicillin for about 10 days; stool softener for painful defecation.

**Step 4** **Follow up:** review response; consider household contacts and recurrence.

## Board-Style Question — Chapter 14

**A 3-year-old girl has had three episodes of rectal prolapse after straining. She has frequent bulky, greasy stools and is below the 3rd centile for weight. Which investigation is most important?**

- **A.** Barium enema
- **B.** Sweat chloride test
- **C.** MRI of the pelvis
- **D.** Anorectal manometry

**Answer and explanation**

- ❌ **A** A barium enema is not the priority.
- ✅ **B** **Correct.** Recurrent rectal prolapse with steatorrhoea and poor growth suggests cystic fibrosis; a sweat test (or the local CF diagnostic pathway) is essential.
- ❌ **C** MRI is not indicated.
- ❌ **D** Manometry does not address the likely underlying cause.

## Five Most Dangerous Mistakes — Chapter 14

1. ⚠️ Missing intussusception by calling it gastroenteritis.
2. ⚠️ Treating streptococcal perianal dermatitis as candida for weeks.
3. ⚠️ Under-treating constipation and blaming the child for soiling.
4. ⚠️ Missing cystic fibrosis behind recurrent prolapse.
5. ⚠️ Failing to act on — or over-interpreting — anogenital findings when abuse is a possibility.
