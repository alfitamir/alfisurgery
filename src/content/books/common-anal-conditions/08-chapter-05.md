## 1. The Cryptoglandular Rule: One Disease, Two Stages

Most anorectal abscesses and fistulas are the acute and chronic phases of the same infection, which starts in an anal gland opening at the dentate line (ASCRS 2022).

- A fistula is already present in a proportion of patients at the time of drainage.

- After drainage, a fistula or recurrent abscess develops in a substantial minority — reported rates range widely with follow-up (in the PPAC2 trial, 11–15% had a fistula within months).

- **Warn every patient before drainage** that a later fistula reflects the biology of the gland, not a surgical error.

## 2. The Four Abscess Spaces — and Why Deep Pus Does Not Fluctuate

## 3. Abscess Management Rules (ASCRS 2022; PPAC2 2022)

1. **Drain promptly — never wait for fluctuance** [S][C]. Fever plus severe localised perianal or rectal pain means drainage today.
2. **Incise close to the anal verge** [C], so that if a fistula forms its external opening lies near the sphincter, not far out on the buttock.
3. **No routine packing** [S]. In the PPAC2 randomised trial (433 adults, 50 UK hospitals), packing produced higher pain scores (mean maximum VAS 38.2 vs 28.2; P < 0.0001) without reducing fistula formation (15% vs 11%) or recurrence. Pack only for haemostasis, then use an absorbent dressing. (PPAC2 excluded horseshoe abscesses, known fistulas, and Fournier’s gangrene.)
4. **Antibiotics are adjuncts, not substitutes** [C]. After drainage, give antibiotics selectively for surrounding cellulitis, systemic signs of infection, or immunosuppression (ASCRS 2022); many clinicians also use them in poorly controlled diabetes or with prosthetic heart valves [L].
5. **Do not probe aggressively during acute inflammation** [C] — false tracts through the sphincter are easily created.
6. **Imaging (MRI, CT, or endoanal ultrasound)** helps when the abscess is deep, recurrent, horseshoe, or Crohn’s-related, or the diagnosis is uncertain — but **never delays drainage of a clinically obvious abscess** [C].
7. **Immunosuppressed and neutropenic patients** may have little pus or fluctuance. Arrange early surgical review; the neutropenic precautions in Chapter 06 never justify delaying surgical assessment [C].

## 4. Fistula-in-Ano for Non-Surgeons

### Goodsall’s rule — predicting the internal opening [C]

- Draw a transverse line across the anus with the patient in lithotomy. **Anterior** external openings usually track radially, straight to the nearest crypt. **Posterior** openings usually curve to the posterior midline.

- Exceptions are common: anterior openings more than about 3 cm from the verge may curve posteriorly, and the rule is less reliable in women and in Crohn’s disease.

### Parks classification and sphincter risk

**Table 5.2 — Parks classification**

| Type              | Course                                                   | Sphincter risk and usual approach                                                                                                |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Intersphincteric  | Between internal and external sphincters                 | 🟢 Usually “simple”: fistulotomy is often curative when continence is normal.                                                    |
| Trans-sphincteric | Crosses both sphincters into the ischioanal fat          | 🟡 Depends on how much external sphincter is involved — low tracts may be laid open; high tracts need sphincter-sparing surgery. |
| Suprasphincteric  | Loops above the puborectalis                             | 🔴 High incontinence risk — never laid open.                                                                                     |
| Extrasphincteric  | From rectum through the levators, outside the sphincters | 🔴 Think pelvic sepsis, Crohn’s disease, trauma, or malignancy.                                                                  |

<small>Parks’ original 1976 series (a referral population) reported roughly 45% intersphincteric, 30% trans-sphincteric, 20% suprasphincteric, and 5% extrasphincteric; suprasphincteric tracts are less frequent in most contemporary series.</small>

### “Complex” fistula — refer for imaging and a sphincter-sparing plan (ASCRS 2022)

- Involves a substantial portion of the external sphincter (commonly > 30%) or is suprasphincteric or extrasphincteric.

- Anterior fistula in a woman; multiple tracts; recurrent fistula; pre-existing incontinence; previous pelvic radiation; Crohn’s disease.

- **Pelvic MRI (fistula protocol)** is the usual first imaging test for complex, recurrent, or Crohn’s-related fistulas.

### Why setons are a bridge, not a failure

A loose draining seton keeps the tract open, drains sepsis continuously, and allows inflammation to settle before a staged sphincter-sparing repair, such as ligation of the intersphincteric fistula tract (LIFT), endorectal advancement flap, fistulectomy with primary sphincter repair, or laser or video-assisted techniques. Fistula plugs and fibrin glue have low and variable success (ASCRS 2022). Fistulectomy with primary sphincter repair has been reported by the author’s group in a single-centre series of 71 patients with one recurrence (Lasheen et al. 2016) — encouraging but low-certainty evidence [U].

## 5. Surgical Emergency: Fournier’s Gangrene

Fournier’s gangrene is a polymicrobial necrotising fasciitis of the perineal, perianal, and genital region. It spreads along fascial planes — very rapid spread (up to 2–3 cm per hour) has been reported — and mortality rises with every hour of delay to debridement (WSES-AAST 2021; WSES/SIS-E 2018).

### Cardinal clues — never wait for all of them

- **Pain out of proportion** to visible skin changes. Early skin may show only subtle redness, oedema, or a bronze tinge; numbness appears late as cutaneous nerves die.

- Subcutaneous **crepitus**, foul “dishwater” discharge, dusky or violaceous skin, bullae, or rapidly spreading erythema.

- **Systemic toxicity:** fever or hypothermia, tachycardia, hypotension, raised lactate, acute kidney injury, new confusion.

- **Risk multipliers:** diabetes; **SGLT2 inhibitors** (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin, ertugliflozin — subject of a 2018 FDA safety warning; Bersoff-Matcha et al. 2019); alcohol misuse; cirrhosis; obesity; HIV or other immunosuppression; chemotherapy; recent perineal surgery, urethral instrumentation, or stricture.

- **A low LRINEC score or an unremarkable CT never excludes necrotising infection** — this is a clinical diagnosis (WSES-AAST 2021) [C].

## 7. Role-Targeted Action Boxes

> **🚑 ER DOCTOR ACTION BOX**
>
> - **Normal skin + severe deep rectal pain + fever = intersphincteric or supralevator abscess** until proven otherwise. Do not discharge as “proctalgia” or “internal piles”; call surgery for examination under anaesthesia.
> - **Ask every diabetic with perineal pain whether they take an SGLT2 inhibitor,** and look specifically for crepitus and skin discolouration.

> **🩺 FAMILY MEDICINE / GP CLINIC BOX**
>
> - **Stop daily ribbon-gauze packing of drained abscesses.** Advise showers or warm-water sitz baths and an absorbent pad (PPAC2).
> - **Never treat a perianal swelling with antibiotics alone.** Refer the same day.
> - **Arrange follow-up after drainage:** persistent discharge or a recurring lump suggests a fistula.

> **🏥 ER & WARD NURSE CARE AND SBAR BOX**
>
> - **Mark and time the erythema border** with a skin pen on arrival in any patient with perianal or perineal cellulitis.
> - **Escalate immediately by SBAR** if redness spreads beyond the line, new dusky skin, bullae, or crepitus appear, or the patient has respiratory rate ≥ 22/min, systolic BP ≤ 100 mmHg, or new confusion (qSOFA), or a NEWS2 score at the local trigger level [L].

> **💊 CROSS-SPECIALTY PRESCRIBING TRAP**
>
> - **SGLT2 inhibitors:** stop immediately when Fournier’s gangrene is suspected; check blood ketones and anion gap even if glucose is near normal. Any restart is a specialist decision.
> - **Ceftriaxone alone is not adequate** empirical cover for necrotising perineal infection.

> **⛔ NEVER DO THIS**
>
> - Never treat a perianal abscess with antibiotics alone.
> - Never pack a drained abscess cavity routinely.
> - Never lay open a high, anterior-female, recurrent, or Crohn’s fistula; never use a tight “cutting” seton as a quick fix.
> - Never delay Fournier’s surgery for imaging or a scoring system.
> - Never probe aggressively for an internal opening in acute sepsis.

> **📝 COMMON DOCUMENTATION ERROR**
>
> - **“Abscess drained”** without the anatomical site, distance from the verge, volume, whether an internal opening was seen, culture (if recurrent or immunosuppressed), and fistula counselling.
> - **Fournier’s gangrene without time stamps:** arrival, surgical call, antibiotics, theatre start.

**📨 REFERRAL URGENCY BOX**

| Urgency      | Clinical trigger                                                                                              | Who and how fast                           |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| 🔴 EMERGENCY | Suspected Fournier’s gangrene; abscess with septic shock                                                      | Senior surgeon and theatre now             |

## The Patient in Front of You — Chapter 05 Mini-Case

> **🧑‍⚕️ THE PATIENT IN FRONT OF YOU**
>
> A 56-year-old man with type 2 diabetes on empagliflozin 10 mg daily presents at 02:00 with 10/10 perineal and scrotal pain. Temperature 38.6 °C, heart rate 118/min, blood pressure 96/60 mmHg, glucose 9.2 mmol/L (165 mg/dL). The perianal skin shows only mild bronze induration and a 2 cm dusky patch, but gentle palpation reveals crepitus. The resident has requested a contrast CT (2-hour wait) and given IV ceftriaxone.

**Step 1** **Cancel the delay.** Pain out of proportion, crepitus, tachycardia, and hypotension in a diabetic on an SGLT2 inhibitor = Fournier’s gangrene.

**Step 2** **Launch the first-hour bundle.** Call the senior surgeon and theatre now; resuscitate; stop empagliflozin and check ketones and anion gap; broaden antibiotics to a carbapenem or piperacillin–tazobactam plus MRSA cover as indicated plus clindamycin, per local protocol.

**Step 3** **Source control.** Straight to theatre for radical debridement, then critical care and a planned re-look within 24–48 hours.

**Step 4** **Document times** of each step.

## Board-Style Question — Chapter 05

**During drainage of an acute perianal abscess in a 38-year-old man, the surgeon finds a high trans-sphincteric fistula crossing more than half of the external sphincter. What is the most appropriate management?**

- **A.** Immediate fistulotomy of the whole tract and daily tight iodoform packing
- **B.** Loose draining seton, no routine cavity packing, and a planned staged sphincter-sparing repair
- **C.** Fibrin glue injection and primary skin closure under antibiotic cover
- **D.** Tight cutting seton to divide the external sphincter over 48 hours

**Answer and explanation**

- ❌ **A** Dividing a high tract risks permanent incontinence, and routine packing adds pain without benefit.
- ✅ **B** **Correct.** A loose seton controls sepsis and allows a later sphincter-sparing repair (ASCRS 2022). Omitting routine packing reduces pain without increasing fistula or recurrence (PPAC2).
- ❌ **C** Fibrin glue has low and variable success, especially in acute sepsis; closing an abscess cavity invites recurrence.
- ❌ **D** Tight or cutting setons carry a substantial risk of sphincter injury and incontinence.

## Five Most Dangerous Mistakes — Chapter 05

1. ⚠️ Discharging a febrile patient with deep rectal pain and normal skin.
2. ⚠️ Prescribing antibiotics instead of draining an abscess.
3. ⚠️ Sending a patient with crepitus and hypotension for CT before calling the surgeon.
4. ⚠️ Missing euglycaemic ketoacidosis in a patient on an SGLT2 inhibitor.
5. ⚠️ Laying open a complex or Crohn’s fistula and causing incontinence.
