## 1. Classification: Goligher Plus BPRST

The Goligher classification grades **internal prolapse only**. Patients also present with bleeding alone, troublesome external tags, and acute thrombosis. The BPRST classification (Sobrado et al. 2020, 2021) adds these dimensions. Use both: Goligher for communication, BPRST to guide treatment.

**Table 3.1 — Goligher classification and usual first-line approach**

| Grade | Prolapse behaviour | Usual first-line approach (ASCRS 2024; AGA 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| I | Bleeding without prolapse | 🟢 Diet, fibre, fluids, toilet habits; office procedure if symptoms persist. |
| II | Prolapses on straining; reduces spontaneously | 🟢 Conservative first; office procedure if persistent — rubber band ligation (RBL) is the most effective office option [S]. |
| III | Prolapses; needs manual reduction | 🟡 RBL in selected patients; surgical consultation if banding fails or there is a significant external component (AGA 2026); excisional haemorrhoidectomy for symptomatic combined internal and external disease (ASCRS 2024). |
| IV | Permanently prolapsed or irreducible | 🔴 Surgical haemorrhoidectomy (AGA 2026); **not** for office banding. |

### BPRST components and stages

- **B — Bleeding:** B0 none · B1 present.

- **P — Prolapse:** P0 none · P1 one pile prolapses · P2 two or more piles prolapse.

- **R — Reduction:** R0 spontaneous · R1 manual · R2 irreducible.

- **S — Skin tags:** S0 none · S1 symptomatic tags.

- **T — Thrombosis:** T0 none · T1 acute thrombosis.

**Table 3.2 — BPRST stages and strategy**

| BPRST stage | Criteria | Therapeutic strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Stage I | Bleeding only (B1, no prolapse) | 🟢 Lifestyle and fibre; office procedure (RBL, IRC, or sclerotherapy) if refractory. |
| Stage II | Prolapse (P1 or P2) that reduces spontaneously or manually (R0 or R1); no symptomatic tags or thrombosis | 🟡 Stage I measures plus RBL as first-line office treatment; non-excisional procedures (for example Doppler-guided artery ligation with mucopexy) in specialist hands. |
| Stage III | Any irreducible prolapse (R2), symptomatic skin tags (S1), or acute thrombosis (T1) | 🔴 **Do not band or coagulate.** Banding irreducible or thrombosed tissue causes severe pain, necrosis, and sepsis. Requires surgical management (excisional haemorrhoidectomy, or targeted thrombus or tag excision). |

<small>Evidence note: BPRST is a pragmatic staging system from one Brazilian group; external validation is limited [U]. Stapled haemorrhoidopexy is not recommended routinely as first-line surgery because of higher recurrence and rare serious complications (rectovaginal fistula, perforation, staple-line stricture, chronic tenesmus) (ASCRS 2024; AGA 2026).</small>

## 2. First-Line Medical and Lifestyle Care (Every Grade, Every Patient)

1. **Fibre and fluids [S].** Aim for **25–35 g of total dietary fibre per day**. If a supplement is needed, start psyllium (ispaghula) at about 3.5 g once or twice daily and titrate over 1–2 weeks, each dose with a full glass of water. A meta-analysis of randomised trials found fibre roughly halved the risk of persisting symptoms (RR 0.53) and bleeding (RR 0.50) (Alonso-Coello et al. 2006). Warn about temporary bloating.
2. **Toilet habits [C].** Respond to the first urge, avoid straining, and keep toilet visits to a few minutes — no phone or reading on the toilet. A footstool may help some patients [U].
3. **Oral flavonoids (micronised purified flavonoid fraction, diosmin–hesperidin) [C][U].** May shorten acute bleeding and symptom episodes; licensed in many countries but not as a drug in the United States. Use short courses; evidence quality is modest.
4. **Topical treatments [C].** Anaesthetics, astringents, corticosteroids, and vasoactive agents may give symptom relief but have little efficacy data. **Limit topical corticosteroids to 2 weeks at a time** (AGA 2026) to avoid atrophy, dermatitis, and infection.
5. **Sitz baths [U].** Often advised, though data are limited (AGA 2026). Plain warm water is sufficient; avoid antiseptics such as chloroxylenol (Dettol) or povidone-iodine, which irritate anoderm.

## 3. Office Procedures: Matching the Tool to the Patient

Offer an office procedure when grade I–III internal haemorrhoids continue to bleed or prolapse after 4–6 weeks of conservative care (ASCRS 2024; AGA 2026). **Obtain informed consent that includes the small risk of pelvic sepsis, and tell the patient to attend the ER immediately if warning symptoms develop** (AGA 2026 [C]).

**Table 3.3 — Office procedures**

| Procedure | Best indication | Antithrombotics and coagulopathy | Complications and watch-points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rubber band ligation (RBL) | 🟢 Grade I–III refractory to conservative care. **Most effective office treatment**, with longer-lasting control of prolapse and bleeding (ASCRS 2024 [S]; AGA 2026). | 🟡 Risk of **delayed bleeding (typically days 5–10)** when the banded pedicle sloughs. Plan with the prescribing team: many units continue aspirin alone; warfarin, DOACs, and P2Y12 inhibitors are managed by local peri-procedural protocol [C][L]. If anticoagulation cannot be interrupted, prefer IRC or sclerotherapy. | ⚠️ Immediate severe pain (band near or below dentate line); delayed bleeding; urinary retention.<br>🚨 **Pelvic sepsis triad:** worsening pain + fever + urinary difficulty (usually within the first week) = emergency. |
| Infrared coagulation (IRC) | 🟡 Small, bleeding grade I–II haemorrhoids. Similar short-term results to RBL; less durable for prolapse (AGA 2026). | 🟢 **Preferred over banding in significant coagulopathy** — platelets < 50 × 10⁹/L or INR > 2.0 (AGA 2026, discussing cirrhosis) [C]. | Mild discomfort; may need repeat sessions. |
| Injection sclerotherapy | 🟡 Bleeding grade I–II haemorrhoids. | 🟢 An option when antithrombotics cannot be stopped (no slough ulcer) [C]. | Higher recurrence than RBL; repeat sessions common. Rare serious complications from misplaced injection (prostatic or urethral injury, abscess, necrosis). |

## 4. Special Populations

**Table 3.4 — Special populations**

| Group | What to do | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Active Crohn’s disease or ulcerative colitis | Delay haemorrhoid procedures until complete remission; treat the IBD first (Chapter 09). | AGA 2026 [C] |
| Pregnancy | Conservative management (fibre, constipation treatment, topical agents); consider banding or IRC after delivery if symptoms persist or further pregnancies are planned (Chapter 13). | AGA 2026 [C] |
| Cirrhosis or portal hypertension | Distinguish haemorrhoids from **rectal varices**. Haemorrhoids can be banded or coagulated; IRC preferred if significant coagulopathy. Varices need specialist management. | AGA 2026 [C] |
| Antithrombotic therapy | Agree a peri-procedural plan with the prescriber before banding or surgery. | [C][L] |
| Neutropenia or profound immunosuppression | Avoid elective office procedures; high sepsis risk (Chapter 06). | [C] |
| People living with HIV | Examine carefully for condylomata, HSIL, and anal cancer before attributing symptoms to haemorrhoids (Chapters 07, 11). | [C] |

## 5. Before Referral for Surgery, and After Haemorrhoidectomy

### Pre-referral checklist

- **Risk-based colonic evaluation** (ASCRS 2024): age at or above the national screening start age without up-to-date screening (45 years in the United States; 50 in many other countries), iron-deficiency anaemia, altered bowel habit, weight loss, family history of colorectal cancer or IBD, or bleeding atypical for haemorrhoids → colonoscopy first (Chapter 08).

- **IBD status** — defer elective haemorrhoid treatment if active (AGA 2026).

- **Antithrombotic plan, baseline continence, and pregnancy plans** documented.

### What to expect after haemorrhoidectomy (MDH energy-sealing standard) [C]

- **Multimodal analgesia:** regular paracetamol plus an NSAID unless contraindicated; topical metronidazole or diltiazem may reduce pain in some protocols; minimise opioids and always co-prescribe a laxative.

- **Bowel care:** stool softener (macrogol or lactulose) and fibre from day 1.

- **Sitz baths:** plain warm water, 10–15 minutes, 2–3 times daily and after bowel movements.

- **Expected:** spotting with bowel movements for several weeks.

- **Seek help immediately for:** bleeding soaking more than one pad per hour or passing clots; inability to pass urine for 6–8 hours, or painful bladder fullness; temperature **above 38.0 °C**; or pain that keeps worsening despite regular analgesia (Appendix D).

## 6. Dangerous Mimics — Things You Might Band by Mistake

**Table 3.5 — Haemorrhoid mimics**

| Mimic | Clue | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Rectal varices | Cirrhosis or portal hypertension; bluish, compressible channels above the dentate line | Banding or injection can cause major bleeding — specialist management |
| Low rectal polyp or cancer | Firm, irregular, or friable; does not compress; altered bowel habit | Needs biopsy and staging, not a band |
| Full-thickness rectal prolapse | Concentric rings; lax tone | Banding is ineffective; needs colorectal assessment |
| Hypertrophied anal papilla / fibroepithelial polyp | Firm, pale, arises at the dentate line; often with chronic fissure | Treat the fissure; excise if symptomatic |
| Anal condylomata | Warty papules; HPV risk factors | Sexual health review; HSIL risk (Chapters 07, 11) |
| Anorectal melanoma | Pigmented or amelanotic polypoid mass | Urgent biopsy |
| Crohn’s skin tags | Oedematous, bluish, “elephant-ear” tags with fissures or diarrhoea | Excision causes non-healing wounds (Chapter 09) |

## 7. Role-Targeted Action Boxes

> **🚑 ER DOCTOR ACTION BOX**
>
> - **Two post-banding emergencies never to miss:**
> - **1 — Pelvic sepsis (usually within the first week): worsening perineal or pelvic pain + fever + urinary difficulty** (sometimes nausea or faecal incontinence). Start the sepsis bundle, give broad-spectrum IV antibiotics covering Gram-negative and anaerobic organisms per local policy, and call the on-call surgeon for urgent examination under anaesthesia (ASCRS 2024; AGA 2026). Imaging must not delay surgical review.
> - **2 — Delayed haemorrhage (typically days 5–10):** caused by sloughing of the banded pedicle, especially if antithrombotics were restarted. Resuscitate; check full blood count, coagulation, and group-and-screen; call surgery for anoscopy and haemostasis. Balloon tamponade can bridge to definitive control [C].

> **🩺 FAMILY MEDICINE / GP CLINIC BOX**
>
> - **Give a real conservative trial first** (4–6 weeks of fibre, fluids, and toilet discipline) before referring grade I–II haemorrhoids.
> - **Know when banding is off the table:** irreducible (R2) or thrombosed (T1) haemorrhoids, external tags (S1), significant coagulopathy, active IBD, and pregnancy.
> - **List all antithrombotics** (aspirin, P2Y12 inhibitors, warfarin, DOACs) in the referral letter.

> **🏥 ER & WARD NURSE CARE AND SBAR BOX**
>
> - **Voiding after surgery:** encourage oral fluids and voiding; avoid excessive IV fluids once the patient is drinking [C]. If there is no void by 6–8 hours, or the bladder scan exceeds 400 mL with discomfort, escalate by SBAR for catheterisation.
> - **Discharge teach-back:** the patient should repeat the three return triggers — heavy bleeding, cannot pass urine, fever or worsening pain.

> **💊 CROSS-SPECIALTY PRESCRIBING TRAP**
>
> - **Repeat prescriptions of steroid haemorrhoid creams or suppositories:** cap at 2 weeks at a time (AGA 2026). Steroids thin the anoderm, cause dermatitis and fungal infection, and never shrink a prolapsing cushion.
> - **Opioids after banding or surgery without a laxative** produce hard stool that tears healing wounds.

> **⛔ NEVER DO THIS**
>
> - Never band irreducible (R2), acutely thrombosed (T1), or external haemorrhoids or skin tags.
> - Never place a band at or below the dentate line.
> - Never band a suspected rectal varix outside specialist care.
> - Never prescribe topical steroids for more than 2 weeks at a time.
> - Never attribute iron-deficiency anaemia to haemorrhoids before the colon (and, where indicated, the upper GI tract) has been examined.

> **📝 COMMON DOCUMENTATION ERROR**
>
> - **“Piles”** without a grade, anoscopy findings, and alarm-feature screen.
> - **No antithrombotic plan** (which drug, stop date, restart date) before banding.
> - **Consent without pelvic sepsis** listed as a risk, and no written return instructions (AGA 2026).

**📨 REFERRAL URGENCY BOX**

| Urgency | Clinical trigger | Who and how fast |
|---|---|---|
| 🔴 EMERGENCY | Post-banding pelvic sepsis triad; post-procedure bleeding with tachycardia, hypotension, or falling haemoglobin | On-call surgeon now; resuscitate |
| 🔴 SAME-DAY | Strangulated or gangrenous grade IV haemorrhoids; urinary retention after a procedure | Surgical review before discharge |
| 🟡 URGENT | Bleeding with any alarm feature | Colonoscopy pathway (Chapter 08) |
| 🟢 ROUTINE | Grade I–III failing 4–6 weeks of conservative care; grade III–IV with external component | Office-procedure or surgical clinic |

## The Patient in Front of You — Chapter 03 Mini-Case

> **🧑‍⚕️ THE PATIENT IN FRONT OF YOU**
>
> A 49-year-old man comes to the ER 3 days after outpatient rubber band ligation of two grade II internal haemorrhoids. He has escalating deep pelvic pain, a temperature of 38.7 °C, and has been unable to pass urine for 8 hours despite a full bladder. The triage note reads “post-banding discomfort — for analgesia and discharge”.

**Step 1** **Spot the triad.** Worsening pain + fever + urinary retention within the first week after banding is post-banding pelvic sepsis until proven otherwise — rare but potentially fatal.

**Step 2** **Resuscitate now.** Do not reassure or discharge. IV access, blood cultures, lactate, full blood count, and broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (Gram-negative, anaerobic, and Gram-positive cover per local policy). Decompress the bladder with a catheter.

**Step 3** **Escalate.** Call the on-call surgical consultant for same-day examination under anaesthesia, band removal, and drainage or debridement. CT can help define extent but must not delay surgical review.

**Step 4** **Document times:** arrival, antibiotics, surgical call, theatre.

## Board-Style Question — Chapter 03

**A 54-year-old man with cirrhosis and portal hypertension has recurrent painless bright-red bleeding from grade II internal haemorrhoids despite 8 weeks of fibre. Anoscopy confirms internal haemorrhoids and no rectal varices; colonoscopy 6 months ago showed no other source. Platelets are 38 × 10⁹/L and INR is 2.3. Which office treatment is preferred?**

- **A.** Rubber band ligation of all three columns
- **B.** Infrared coagulation
- **C.** Stapled haemorrhoidopexy
- **D.** Bedside excision of the haemorrhoids

**Answer and explanation**

- ❌ **A** Banding risks serious delayed bleeding when the pedicle sloughs; the AGA advises against it in significant coagulopathy.
- ✅ **B** **Correct.** The AGA 2026 Clinical Practice Update states that haemorrhoids in cirrhosis can be treated with banding or IRC, but that **IRC is preferred in significant coagulopathy** (platelets < 50 × 10⁹/L or INR > 2.0), and that portal hypertension alone should not change this approach.
- ❌ **C** Stapled haemorrhoidopexy is not a first-line operation (ASCRS 2024) and carries prohibitive bleeding risk here.
- ❌ **D** Excision is a surgical, not an office, treatment and is inappropriate for bleeding grade II haemorrhoids in uncorrected coagulopathy.

## Five Most Dangerous Mistakes — Chapter 03

1. ⚠️ Discharging a patient with the pelvic sepsis triad as “post-banding discomfort”.
2. ⚠️ Treating bleeding “piles” in a patient with iron-deficiency anaemia without colon evaluation.
3. ⚠️ Banding a thrombosed, irreducible, or external haemorrhoid — or a varix.
4. ⚠️ Banding a patient on a DOAC or P2Y12 inhibitor without a peri-procedural plan.
5. ⚠️ Renewing steroid haemorrhoid creams month after month.
