# Chapter 11 — Sexually Transmitted Infections, Proctitis, and Anorectal Sexual Health

_Purpose: ask every patient with anorectal symptoms about sexual exposure — kindly and routinely — because gonorrhoea, chlamydia (including LGV), syphilis, herpes, mpox, and HIV all present as “piles”, “fissures”, or “proctitis”._

## 1. The Non-Judgemental Sexual History

Take the history in private, without relatives present, using neutral language and the patient’s own terms. Explain why you are asking: _“I ask everyone with these symptoms about sex, because some infections look exactly like piles or a fissure.”_ Use a professional interpreter, never a family member. Documentation and reporting duties vary between countries — follow local law and professional confidentiality codes [L].

**Table 11.1 — The “5 Ps” (CDC 2021)**

| Domain               | Example question                                                                                                |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Partners             | “In the past 3 months, how many sexual partners have you had? Were they men, women, or both?”                   |
| Practices            | “Have you had receptive anal sex (a partner’s penis, fingers, or objects in your anus)? Oral sex? Vaginal sex?” |
| Protection from STIs | “How often do you use condoms? Do you use HIV PrEP?”                                                            |
| Past history of STIs | “Have you ever been diagnosed with an STI? When were you last tested for HIV?”                                  |
| Pregnancy intention  | “Could you be pregnant? Are you trying to become pregnant?”                                                     |

## 2. Pathogens, Presentations, and Tests

**Table 11.2 — Infectious causes of anorectal symptoms**

| Infection                                                         | Typical anorectal presentation                                                                                               | Key test                                                           |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Gonorrhoea                                                        | Proctitis: mucopurulent discharge, tenesmus, pain; often asymptomatic                                                        | Rectal NAAT                                                        |
| Chlamydia (non-LGV)                                               | Mild proctitis or none                                                                                                       | Rectal NAAT                                                        |
| Lymphogranuloma venereum (LGV, serovars L1–L3)                    | Severe proctocolitis: bloody discharge, tenesmus, ulceration, fever, inguinal nodes — **can mimic Crohn’s disease**          | Rectal NAAT; LGV typing where available                            |
| Syphilis                                                          | Primary chancre (often painless, indurated ulcer — can mimic a fissure); secondary condylomata lata, rash on palms and soles | Treponemal and non-treponemal serology; lesion PCR where available |
| Herpes simplex (HSV)                                              | Very painful vesicles or ulcers, severe proctitis, fever; may cause urinary retention or sacral radiculopathy                | HSV PCR from lesion                                                |
| Mpox                                                              | Painful anorectal lesions and proctitis, often with fever and lymphadenopathy                                                | Mpox PCR from lesion                                               |
| HPV                                                               | Anogenital warts; HSIL and cancer (Chapter 07)                                                                               | Clinical; biopsy if atypical                                       |
| HIV                                                               | Acute seroconversion (fever, rash, sore throat, nodes); co-infection with all of the above                                   | Fourth-generation antigen/antibody test                            |
| Enteric (_Shigella_, _Campylobacter_, amoebiasis, _C. difficile_) | Bloody diarrhoea, cramps, proctocolitis                                                                                      | Stool culture or PCR (with susceptibility testing)                 |

<small>Test every exposed site (rectum, pharynx, urethra or vagina). Also test for HIV, syphilis, hepatitis B and C, check hepatitis A and B immunity, and offer a pregnancy test where relevant.</small>

## 3. Treatment (CDC 2021) [S]

**Table 11.3 — Adult treatment regimens (check national guidance [L])**

| Situation                                       | Recommended regimen                                                                                           | Notes                                                                                                                                                                     |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Acute proctitis — empirical, before results** | Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM single dose (1 g if ≥ 150 kg) **plus** doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for 7 days | Extend doxycycline to **21 days** if LGV is suspected (bloody discharge, ulcers, or tenesmus with positive rectal chlamydia). Add HSV treatment if ulcers suggest herpes. |
| Gonorrhoea (uncomplicated)                      | Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM single dose                                                                             | Some national guidelines use 1 g [L]. Retest at 3 months.                                                                                                                 |
| Rectal chlamydia                                | Doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for 7 days                                                              | Pregnancy: azithromycin 1 g orally single dose.                                                                                                                           |
| LGV                                             | Doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for 21 days                                                             | Pregnancy: specialist regimen.                                                                                                                                            |
| Primary, secondary, or early latent syphilis    | Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM single dose                                                      | Penicillin allergy (not pregnant): doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 14 days. Pregnancy: penicillin (desensitise if allergic).                                           |
| First-episode genital or anal herpes            | Valaciclovir 1 g orally twice daily for 7–10 days (or aciclovir 400 mg three times daily)                     | Severe proctitis or immunosuppression: specialist; IV aciclovir if severe.                                                                                                |
| Disseminated gonococcal infection               | Admit; ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily                                                                               | Fever, arthritis, tenosynovitis, pustular skin lesions.                                                                                                                   |
| Mpox                                            | Supportive care; analgesia; stool softeners                                                                   | Antivirals only via specialist or public-health pathways [U].                                                                                                             |
| _Shigella_ and other enteric infections         | Guided by culture and susceptibility                                                                          | Antimicrobial resistance is common [L].                                                                                                                                   |

> **ℹ️ AFTER EVERY DIAGNOSIS**
>
> - **Partner notification:** treat or test partners; expedited partner therapy only where legal [L].
> - **Abstain** from sex for 7 days after treatment and until partners are treated.
> - **Retest** at 3 months after gonorrhoea, chlamydia, or trichomonas.

## 4. When to Suspect LGV, HIV, Syphilis, or Disseminated Infection

- **LGV:** proctitis with bloody discharge, ulceration, fever, or nodes — especially in men who have sex with men; colitis that “looks like Crohn’s” in someone with sexual risk.

- **HIV:** any new STI, recurrent STIs, acute febrile illness with rash and nodes, unusual or severe infections, or anal HSIL/cancer.

- **Syphilis:** a painless anal ulcer, a rash on palms and soles, condylomata lata, or neurological, ocular, or hearing symptoms (urgent specialist review).

- **Disseminated gonococcal infection:** fever with migratory arthritis, tenosynovitis, or pustular skin lesions — admit.

## 5. Prevention and Anal Cancer Protection

- **Condoms;** **HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)** for people at ongoing risk.

- **Doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis (doxy-PEP) [S]:** 200 mg once, within 72 hours of condomless sex, offered after shared decision-making to men who have sex with men and transgender women with a bacterial STI in the past 12 months; maximum 200 mg in 24 hours; STI testing every 3–6 months (CDC 2024).

- **Vaccines:** HPV (Chapter 07), hepatitis A and B, and mpox vaccine for eligible people per national programmes [L].

- **Anal cancer screening** for eligible high-risk groups (IANS 2024; Chapter 07).

**Algorithm 11.1 — Rapid STI and proctitis pathway**

```text
[ANORECTAL PAIN, DISCHARGE, TENESMUS, BLEEDING, OR ULCER]
|
+-- Private, non-judgemental sexual history (5 Ps)
|   + examine: ulcers, warts, discharge, groin nodes, rash
|
+-- Systemically unwell? (fever with arthritis or rash,
|   severe pain, urinary retention, sepsis)
|     -> same-day review; admit if DGI, severe HSV, or
|        sepsis; exclude abscess (Chapter 05)
|
+-- TEST: rectal NAAT (GC/CT; LGV typing if CT positive),
|   syphilis serology, HIV, HSV/mpox PCR from lesions,
|   stool tests if diarrhoea, hepatitis B/C, pregnancy
|
+-- PROCTITIS (discharge, tenesmus, pain)
|     -> treat NOW, before results (CDC 2021):
|        ceftriaxone 500 mg IM once + doxycycline 100 mg
|        twice daily 7 days (21 days if LGV suspected)
|        + valaciclovir if ulcers suggest herpes
|
+-- ULCER: painful or multiple -> HSV (or mpox)
|          painless or indurated -> syphilis
|          (biopsy if it does not heal: Chapter 07)
|
+-- ALWAYS: partner notification; abstain 7 days and
    until partners treated; PrEP/PEP review; vaccines;
    retest at 3 months
```

| Chronic fissure or anal cancer | Syphilitic chancre                    | Indurated, often painless ulcer; positive serology         |

## 7. Role-Targeted Action Boxes

> **🚑 ER DOCTOR ACTION BOX**
>
> - **Treat proctitis empirically** after swabs; do not wait for results.
> - **Severe HSV proctitis can cause urinary retention** — scan the bladder.
> - **Exposure within the last 72 hours?** Assess for HIV post-exposure prophylaxis now (Chapter 12).

> **🩺 FAMILY MEDICINE / GP CLINIC BOX**
>
> - **Make the sexual history routine** for anorectal complaints — and test all exposed sites.
> - **Offer HIV testing to everyone tested for an STI;** discuss PrEP, doxy-PEP (if eligible), and vaccines.
> - **Book the 3-month retest** at the treatment visit.

> **🏥 ER & WARD NURSE CARE AND SBAR BOX**
>
> - **Protect privacy:** no relatives in the room for the history; offer self-collected swabs where validated.
> - **Teach-back:** medication course, abstinence period, and partner notification.
> - **Flag safeguarding concerns** for anyone under 18 or anyone who may be coerced or exploited.

> **💊 CROSS-SPECIALTY PRESCRIBING TRAP**
>
> - **Doxycycline is avoided in pregnancy** — use azithromycin for chlamydia and seek specialist advice for LGV and syphilis.
> - **Doxycycline:** take with a full glass of water, sitting upright (oesophagitis); photosensitivity.
> - **Haemorrhoid creams for proctitis** delay the diagnosis.

> **⛔ NEVER DO THIS**
>
> - Never treat discharge and tenesmus as “piles” without STI testing.
> - Never take a sexual history in front of relatives or use a family member as interpreter.
> - Never omit an HIV test when an STI is diagnosed or suspected.
> - Never make assumptions about sexual orientation, practices, or relationships.

> **📝 COMMON DOCUMENTATION ERROR**
>
> - **Judgemental or speculative language** about sexual behaviour; record only what is clinically necessary.
> - **No record of sites tested,** partner-notification plan, or retest date.

**📨 REFERRAL URGENCY BOX**

| Urgency      | Clinical trigger                                                                                          | Who and how fast                                              |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 🔴 EMERGENCY | Sepsis; disseminated gonococcal infection with meningitis or endocarditis features                        | Resuscitate; admit; infectious diseases                       |
| 🔴 SAME-DAY  | Severe HSV with urinary retention; DGI; exposure within 72 hours needing HIV PEP; minor at immediate risk | ER or sexual-health service; safeguarding                     |

## The Patient in Front of You — Chapter 11 Mini-Case

> **🧑‍⚕️ THE PATIENT IN FRONT OF YOU**
>
> A 28-year-old man has had 5 days of rectal pain, bloody mucopurulent discharge, and tenesmus. A haemorrhoid cream has not helped. On private questioning he reports receptive anal sex with two male partners in the past month without condoms. He has tender left inguinal nodes; anoscopy shows proctitis with small ulcers.

**Step 1** **Recognise the syndrome:** proctitis with ulceration and nodes — consider LGV, gonorrhoea, syphilis, and HSV.

**Step 2** **Test:** rectal NAAT (with LGV typing), syphilis serology, HIV, HSV PCR from ulcers, hepatitis B and C.

**Step 3** **Treat today:** ceftriaxone 500 mg IM plus doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 21 days (LGV suspected); add valaciclovir if ulcers look herpetic.

**Step 4** **Prevent:** partner notification, 7-day abstinence, PrEP referral, vaccines, retest at 3 months; discuss doxy-PEP at follow-up.

## Board-Style Question — Chapter 11

**A 32-year-old woman who is 16 weeks pregnant has a positive rectal chlamydia NAAT (non-LGV) and no other infections. What is the most appropriate treatment?**

- **A.** Doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days
- **B.** Azithromycin 1 g orally as a single dose
- **C.** Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM only
- **D.** Defer treatment until after delivery

**Answer and explanation**

- ❌ **A** Doxycycline is avoided in pregnancy.
- ✅ **B** **Correct.** CDC 2021 recommends azithromycin 1 g single dose for chlamydia in pregnancy (amoxicillin is an alternative), with a test of cure about 4 weeks later.
- ❌ **C** Ceftriaxone treats gonorrhoea, not chlamydia.
- ❌ **D** Untreated chlamydia risks maternal and neonatal complications.

## Five Most Dangerous Mistakes — Chapter 11

1. ⚠️ Prescribing haemorrhoid cream for STI proctitis.
2. ⚠️ Diagnosing Crohn’s disease in a patient with LGV.
3. ⚠️ Missing HIV because no test was offered.
4. ⚠️ Prescribing doxycycline to a pregnant patient.
5. ⚠️ Taking a sexual history in front of relatives and losing the patient’s trust.
