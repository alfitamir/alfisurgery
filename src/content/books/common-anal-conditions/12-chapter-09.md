# Chapter 09 — Perianal Crohn’s Disease and Inflammatory Bowel Disease

*Purpose: perianal disease is often the first sign of Crohn’s disease — recognise it before anyone divides a sphincter, and manage it as a combined medical–surgical condition.*

## 1. Perianal Manifestations of Crohn’s Disease

Perianal disease affects a substantial proportion of people with Crohn’s disease over time (perianal fistulas alone in up to about one-third) and **may precede intestinal symptoms by months or years** [C].

**Table 9.1 — Perianal manifestations**

| Lesion | Typical features | Practical message |
|---|---|---|
| Skin tags | Large, oedematous, bluish or violaceous (“elephant-ear”) tags | Usually leave alone — excision in active disease can leave non-healing wounds. |
| Fissures and ulcers | Lateral, multiple, deep or cavitating, often relatively painless | Atypical fissure → biopsy and IBD work-up; no sphincterotomy. |
| Abscess | Often recurrent; may be multiple or deep | Drain promptly; look for an associated fistula. |
| Fistula | Frequently complex: high, branching, multiple openings, rectovaginal | MRI and examination under anaesthesia (EUA); loose seton plus medical therapy. |
| Anorectal stricture | Narrowing, obstructive symptoms, painful defecation | Specialist dilatation or surgery; exclude cancer. |
| Malignancy | Squamous cell carcinoma or adenocarcinoma in long-standing fistula or stricture (rare); colorectal cancer in colitis | New pain, mass, bleeding, or mucinous discharge in old disease → MRI, EUA, biopsy (Chapter 07). |

## 2. Idiopathic Versus Crohn’s-Associated Perianal Disease

**Table 9.2 — Distinguishing features**

| Feature | Idiopathic (cryptoglandular / typical) | Crohn’s-associated |
|---|---|---|
| Fissure site | Posterior or anterior midline, single | Lateral, multiple, deep, broad-based |
| Pain | Severe, proportionate to lesion | Often less than expected for the appearance |
| Skin tags | Small, soft, skin-coloured | Large, oedematous, violaceous |
| Fistula | Usually single, low | Often multiple, high, branching; may be rectovaginal |
| Recurrence | Uncommon after appropriate treatment | Recurrent abscesses and fistulas |
| Rectal mucosa | Normal | Proctitis may be present |
| Systemic features | None | Diarrhoea, abdominal pain, weight loss, anaemia, raised CRP, extraintestinal manifestations |

## 3. The “Suspect Crohn’s” Checklist

**Two or more items — or any one strong item (complex fistula, recurrent abscess, lateral or multiple fissures with systemic symptoms) — should trigger an IBD work-up and urgent referral before any sphincter-dividing procedure.**

| ☐ | Feature |
|---|---|
| ☐ | Fissure that is off-midline, multiple, deep, painless, or not healing |
| ☐ | Oedematous, violaceous, or very large skin tags |
| ☐ | Recurrent perianal abscess, more than one external opening, or a complex or recurrent fistula |
| ☐ | Rectovaginal fistula without an obstetric explanation |
| ☐ | Anal or low rectal stricture |
| ☐ | Chronic diarrhoea, urgency, nocturnal stools, or blood and mucus |
| ☐ | Abdominal pain, weight loss, or (in adolescents) growth or pubertal delay |
| ☐ | Iron-deficiency anaemia, raised CRP or platelets, low albumin |
| ☐ | Mouth ulcers, erythema nodosum, pyoderma gangrenosum, red eye (uveitis or episcleritis), or inflammatory arthritis |
| ☐ | Family history of IBD; current smoking (a risk factor for Crohn’s disease) |

## 4. Investigations

**Table 9.3 — Work-up for suspected perianal Crohn’s disease**

| Test | Purpose | Who and caveats |
|---|---|---|
| Full blood count, CRP, ferritin, albumin, renal and liver tests | Inflammation, anaemia, nutrition | Primary care or ER |
| Faecal calprotectin | Distinguishes intestinal inflammation from functional symptoms in adults when cancer is not suspected (NICE DG11) | May be normal in **isolated** perianal disease — a normal result does not exclude Crohn’s disease [C] |
| Stool culture, *C. difficile* testing | Exclude infection, especially in a flare | Primary care or ER |
| HIV test, syphilis serology, rectal STI NAAT | LGV and other STIs can mimic Crohn’s proctitis and perianal disease | Chapter 11 |
| Ileocolonoscopy with biopsies | Confirms diagnosis, extent, and proctitis | Gastroenterology |
| Pelvic MRI (fistula protocol) | Maps fistula tracts and finds occult abscesses (ASCRS 2020) | Specialist request |
| EUA with biopsy, drainage, and seton placement | Reference assessment combined with MRI or endoanal ultrasound | Colorectal surgery |
| Pre-biologic screening (TB interferon-gamma release assay, hepatitis B, HIV, varicella immunity, vaccinations) | Safety before immunosuppression | Gastroenterology; start early to avoid delays |

## 5. Why Sphincter-Dividing Surgery Is Dangerous in Active Crohn’s Disease

- **Wounds heal poorly** in inflamed tissue and can become chronic, painful ulcers.

- **Continence reserve is already reduced:** diarrhoea, urgency, and a stiff, inflamed rectum mean that losing even a small amount of sphincter can cause incontinence.

- **Disease is recurrent and multifocal** — further operations are likely, and each one costs more muscle.

- **Uncontrolled perianal sepsis and repeated surgery can end in a permanent stoma or proctectomy.**

- Fistulotomy is reserved for selected **simple, low** fistulas in patients **without active proctitis**, decided by a colorectal surgeon (ASCRS 2020) [C]. Haemorrhoid procedures are deferred until remission (AGA 2026) [C].

## 6. Combined Medical–Surgical Management

**Table 9.4 — Treatment principles**

| Situation | Action | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Perianal abscess | 🔴 Same-day drainage; place a loose draining seton if a fistula is found; no fistulotomy of complex tracts | ASCRS 2020 [S][C] |
| Undrained sepsis and immunosuppression | **Drain before starting or escalating biologics or steroids** | [C] |
| Active perianal fistula | **Infliximab** is the recommended first-line biologic; adalimumab, ustekinumab, or vedolizumab are suggested alternatives; often combined with an immunomodulator | AGA 2021 [S][C] |
| Maintenance | Maintenance infliximab sustains fistula response better than placebo (ACCENT II) | Sands et al. 2004 [S] |
| Antibiotics (metronidazole, ciprofloxacin) | Symptom control and adjunct to biologics — **not** monotherapy for fistula remission | AGA 2021 [C] |
| Systemic corticosteroids | Do not heal fistulas and worsen sepsis; avoid for perianal disease | [C] |
| Seton removal | Timing is individualised (response on MRI, symptom control, biologic course); guidelines differ | [U] |
| Definitive repair (advancement flap, LIFT) | Considered once sepsis is controlled and proctitis is in remission | ASCRS 2020 [C] |
| Refractory severe disease | Faecal diversion or proctectomy after multidisciplinary discussion | [C] |

## 7. Dangerous Mimics

**Table 9.5 — Conditions that mimic (or coexist with) perianal Crohn’s disease**

| Mimic | Clue | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Lymphogranuloma venereum (LGV) and other STI proctitis | Receptive anal sex; tenesmus, discharge, ulceration; inguinal nodes | Rectal NAAT with LGV typing; treat (Chapter 11) |
| Hidradenitis suppurativa | Multiple nodules and tracts in groin, buttocks, axillae; may coexist with Crohn’s | Dermatology and surgery (Chapter 15) |
| Tuberculosis | Endemic exposure, chronic ulcer or fistula, pulmonary signs | Biopsy, culture, PCR |
| HIV-associated ulcers, CMV, herpes | Immunosuppression, painful ulcers | HIV test, viral PCR, biopsy |
| Anal squamous cell carcinoma | Induration, mass, groin nodes | Biopsy (Chapter 07) |
| Cryptoglandular fistula | Single low tract, normal rectum, no systemic features | Standard management (Chapter 05) |

## 8. Role-Targeted Action Boxes

> **🚑 ER DOCTOR ACTION BOX**
>
> - **Drain the abscess; do not lay open fistulas.** Document the number and position of external openings and request colorectal follow-up.
> - **Patients on biologics, steroids, or immunomodulators may not mount a fever** — examine carefully and lower your threshold for imaging and surgical review.
> - **Avoid starting systemic steroids for a “flare” until perianal sepsis has been excluded.** Send stool for *C. difficile* in every flare.

> **🩺 FAMILY MEDICINE / GP CLINIC BOX**
>
> - **Run the “suspect Crohn’s” checklist** for every patient with a recurrent abscess, atypical fissure, or oedematous tags.
> - **Check faecal calprotectin, bloods, and stool tests,** and refer urgently to gastroenterology and colorectal surgery.
> - **Support smoking cessation** — smoking worsens the course of Crohn’s disease [S].
> - **Update vaccinations early,** before immunosuppression begins; avoid live vaccines once immunosuppressed.

> **🏥 ER & WARD NURSE CARE AND SBAR BOX**
>
> - **Seton care:** keep the area clean with water; some discharge is expected; never pull or tighten the seton; if it falls out, inform the team (usually not an emergency unless pain, swelling, or fever develop).
> - **Skin and continence care:** barrier creams, absorbent pads, and dignity; screen for low mood — perianal Crohn’s disease has a heavy psychological burden.
> - **Escalate by SBAR** for new perianal swelling, fever above 38.0 °C, or rapidly increasing pain in an immunosuppressed patient.

> **💊 CROSS-SPECIALTY PRESCRIBING TRAP**
>
> - **Steroids for perianal sepsis** mask infection and do not heal fistulas.
> - **Loperamide or opioids in severe colitis** can precipitate toxic megacolon.
> - **Repeated courses of antibiotics for recurrent “boils” near the anus** delay the diagnosis of Crohn’s disease.

> **⛔ NEVER DO THIS**
>
> - Never perform or request a sphincterotomy, fistulotomy of a complex tract, haemorrhoidectomy, or tag excision in active Crohn’s disease.
> - Never start or escalate biologics while an abscess is undrained.
> - Never dismiss a third perianal abscess as “bad luck” — think Crohn’s.
> - Never band haemorrhoids in active IBD (AGA 2026).

> **📝 COMMON DOCUMENTATION ERROR**
>
> - **“Recurrent perianal abscess”** with no record of bowel symptoms, weight, or blood tests.
> - **Fistula notes without the number and position of external openings,** seton status, or IBD activity — making MRI and surgical planning harder.

**📨 REFERRAL URGENCY BOX**

| Urgency | Clinical trigger | Who and how fast |
|---|---|---|
| 🔴 EMERGENCY | Perianal sepsis with systemic toxicity or necrotising features; acute severe colitis with systemic toxicity | On-call surgeon and gastroenterologist now |
| 🔴 SAME-DAY | Perianal abscess in known or suspected Crohn’s disease; perianal pain and fever in an immunosuppressed patient | Surgical drainage ± seton; IBD team informed |
| 🟡 URGENT | Positive “suspect Crohn’s” checklist; new or complex fistula; known Crohn’s with a new mass or change | Gastroenterology and colorectal surgery (typically within 2 weeks); MRI |
| 🟢 ROUTINE | Stable known Crohn’s with a quiescent seton | Scheduled IBD and colorectal review |

## The Patient in Front of You — Chapter 09 Mini-Case

> **🧑‍⚕️ THE PATIENT IN FRONT OF YOU**
>
> A 23-year-old woman attends with her third perianal abscess in 12 months; the previous two were drained in the ER. There are now two external openings at the left lateral and right posterior perianal skin, with large oedematous tags. She reports loose stools four times daily for 6 months and 5 kg of weight loss. Haemoglobin 108 g/L, CRP 38 mg/L, platelets 480 × 10⁹/L.

**Step 1** **Suspect Crohn’s disease:** recurrent abscesses, multiple openings, oedematous tags, diarrhoea, weight loss, anaemia, and raised inflammatory markers.

**Step 2** **Control sepsis today:** same-day drainage with a loose draining seton — no fistulotomy; biopsy and cultures.

**Step 3** **Investigate:** faecal calprotectin, stool tests including *C. difficile*, HIV and STI testing, pelvic MRI, and ileocolonoscopy.

**Step 4** **Plan jointly:** urgent gastroenterology review, pre-biologic screening, and infliximab once sepsis is controlled; smoking-cessation support if she smokes.

## Board-Style Question — Chapter 09

**A 31-year-old man with known ileocolonic Crohn’s disease and active proctitis has a complex trans-sphincteric fistula with an associated 3 cm abscess on MRI. What is the best initial management?**

- **A.** Primary fistulotomy of the whole tract
- **B.** Drain the abscess and place a loose seton, then start anti-TNF therapy once sepsis is controlled
- **C.** Oral prednisolone 40 mg daily for 4 weeks
- **D.** Ciprofloxacin alone for 3 months

**Answer and explanation**

- ❌ **A** Fistulotomy of a complex tract in active proctitis risks non-healing wounds and incontinence.
- ✅ **B** **Correct.** Sepsis control (drainage and loose seton) comes first; infliximab is the recommended first-line biologic for perianal fistulising Crohn’s disease (AGA 2021), started once the abscess is drained.
- ❌ **C** Steroids do not heal fistulas and worsen undrained sepsis.
- ❌ **D** Antibiotics are adjuncts, not monotherapy for fistula remission (AGA 2021).

## Five Most Dangerous Mistakes — Chapter 09

1. ⚠️ Performing a sphincterotomy for a lateral fissure that was Crohn’s disease.
2. ⚠️ Laying open a complex Crohn’s fistula and causing incontinence.
3. ⚠️ Starting steroids or biologics with an undrained abscess.
4. ⚠️ Draining recurrent abscesses repeatedly without ever asking about bowel symptoms.
5. ⚠️ Missing carcinoma in a long-standing Crohn’s fistula that changed character.
