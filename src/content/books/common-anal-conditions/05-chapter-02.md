_Purpose: patients call every anorectal symptom “piles” — this chapter gives you a 60-second method to confirm the diagnosis before you write the prescription._

## 2. Look-Alike Showdown 1: Thrombosed External Haemorrhoid Versus Perianal Abscess

Treating an abscess with sitz baths and ointment lets sepsis spread through the sphincter. Opening a thrombosed haemorrhoid with a tiny stab that leaves clot behind tends to re-bleed and re-thrombose.

**Table 2.2 — Thrombosed external haemorrhoid versus perianal abscess**

| Feature                  | Thrombosed external haemorrhoid                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              | Perianal abscess                                                                                                                            |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Onset and course         | Sudden (hours), often after straining, heavy lifting, or prolonged sitting; pain peaks at 24–48 h and usually eases after about 72 h.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        | Gradual over 2–5 days; constant, throbbing, steadily worsening pain.                                                                        |
| Inspection and palpation | Discrete, tense, bluish-purple nodule at the verge, covered by anoderm.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      | Ill-defined red, warm, indurated swelling. **Fluctuance is often absent** in deep ischioanal or intersphincteric sepsis.                    |
| Systemic signs           | 🟢 Afebrile, systemically well.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              | 🔴 Fever, rigors, malaise, tachycardia, raised white cell count.                                                                            |
| Definitive action        | 🟡 **Early (< 72 h) with severe pain:** remove the thrombosed vessel and clot under local anaesthetic. ASCRS 2024 suggests early **excision** for selected patients; the AGA 2026 expert review advises **incision and drainage** — the guidelines differ [U]. Whichever is used, the whole clot must be removed.<br>🟢 **> 72 h or improving:** analgesia, stool softener, warm sitz baths. | 🔴 **Same-day incision and drainage** close to the anal verge (ASCRS 2022). **Never** discharge on antibiotics alone to “see if it points”. |

## 3. Look-Alike Showdown 2: Full-Thickness Rectal Prolapse Versus Prolapsed Internal Haemorrhoids

When a patient arrives with “a large red mass coming out of the bottom”, check the colour, then look at the folds before you touch it.

**Table 2.3 — Rectal prolapse versus prolapsed haemorrhoids**

| Feature              | Full-thickness rectal prolapse                                                                                                                                                      | Prolapsed internal haemorrhoids (grade III–IV)                                                                                                            |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Fold pattern         | 🎯 **Concentric rings** — circular folds stacked like a telescope.                                                                                                                  | 🌸 **Radial grooves** — clefts between discrete cushions, like flower petals.                                                                             |
| Shape and lumen      | Tubular protrusion, often several centimetres long, with a central lumen at the apex.                                                                                               | Lobulated, often oedematous or violaceous cushions; slit-like lumen between them.                                                                         |
| Sphincter tone       | Often lax or patulous; faecal incontinence common.                                                                                                                                  | Normal or tight; spasm may strangulate grade IV haemorrhoids.                                                                                             |
| Emergency management | 🟢 **Viable:** analgesia, osmotic (sugar) reduction if oedematous, gentle reduction, colorectal referral.<br>🔴 **Dusky, black, or perforated:** do not reduce — emergency surgery. | 🟢 **Reducible:** gentle reduction with lubricated gauze and lidocaine gel.<br>🔴 **Strangulated, gangrenous, or irreducible:** same-day surgical review. |

### The granulated-sugar technique for an oedematous, viable rectal prolapse [C]

Evidence: case reports and small series (Myers and Rothenberger 1991; Coburn et al. 1997). It is cheap and low-risk, but not universally successful.

1. **Check viability first.** Pink or red, moist mucosa → proceed. Dusky, black, grey, or perforated, or peritonism → **stop; do not reduce**; call the on-call surgeon.
2. **Position and analgesia.** Left lateral (or prone); IV analgesia or procedural sedation according to local policy.
3. **Cover the prolapse generously** with ordinary granulated table sugar.
4. **Wait about 15–30 minutes**, re-applying as the sugar dissolves; oedema often visibly decreases.
5. **Rinse and reduce.** Rinse with saline, place moist gauze over the apex, and apply steady, gentle pressure to return the bowel through the anal ring. Confirm reduction.
6. **After reduction:** laxatives to avoid straining, analgesia, and colorectal referral. If reduction fails, arrange same-day surgical review.

## 5. Role-Targeted Action Boxes

> **🚑 ER DOCTOR ACTION BOX**
>
> - **Do not make a small stab incision into a thrombosed external haemorrhoid.** Either remove the whole clot and vessel (excision preferred where you are trained, ASCRS 2024) or manage conservatively. Record onset time in hours — it decides management.
> - **Keep granulated sugar accessible** for oedematous, viable rectal prolapse [C]. Never reduce non-viable bowel.
> - **Fever plus perianal pain is an abscess until proven otherwise** — even when the skin looks normal.

> **🩺 FAMILY MEDICINE / GP CLINIC BOX**
>
> - **Look before you prescribe a haemorrhoid cream.** “My piles flared up” plus a temperature above 38.0 °C is an abscess until your eyes and finger prove otherwise — refer the same day.
> - **Perform anoscopy, where available, in every new patient with suspected haemorrhoids** before treatment (AGA 2026 [C]).
> - **A lesion that is hard, ulcerated, pigmented, or not healing after 4–6 weeks of appropriate treatment needs urgent specialist assessment and biopsy.**

> **🏥 ER & WARD NURSE CARE AND SBAR BOX**
>
> - **Before any bedside prolapse reduction,** check and document the mucosal colour. Report dark purple, black, or grey areas, or abdominal pain and guarding, to the doctor immediately — reducing dead bowel into the pelvis causes peritonitis.
> - **Record pain score and temperature** before and after any reduction or drainage procedure.

> **💊 CROSS-SPECIALTY PRESCRIBING TRAP**
>
> - **Antibiotics alone for an undrained abscess** mask surface redness while pus tracks through the sphincter into deeper spaces. Where there is pus, let it out.
> - **Over-the-counter haemorrhoid creams** (local anaesthetic and steroid combinations) used for weeks without an examination can hide a cancer or an abscess and can cause contact dermatitis.

> **⛔ NEVER DO THIS**
>
> - Never reduce black, grey, or necrotic prolapsed bowel.
> - Never discharge a febrile patient with perianal pain on antibiotics alone.
> - Never label a lesion “haemorrhoid” without inspection, DRE, and — where available — anoscopy.
> - Never band or inject a lump that might be a varix, polyp, or tumour.

> **📝 COMMON DOCUMENTATION ERROR**
>
> - **“Haemorrhoids — cream given”** with no examination recorded.
> - **“PR deferred”** without a reason, an alternative assessment, and a date for re-examination or EUA.
> - **“Thrombosed pile”** without temperature, onset time in hours, and a description of the lesion.

**📨 REFERRAL URGENCY BOX**

| Urgency      | Clinical trigger                                                                                                                 | Who and how fast                                                  |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 🔴 EMERGENCY | Gangrenous or perforated prolapse; strangulated haemorrhoids with sepsis                                                         | On-call surgeon now                                               |
| 🔴 SAME-DAY  | Suspected perianal abscess; irreducible viable prolapse; strangulated haemorrhoids without sepsis                                | Surgical review before discharge                                  |
| 🟡 URGENT    | Indurated, ulcerated, pigmented, or non-healing lesion; bleeding with alarm features; rectal prolapse after successful reduction | Biopsy or colonoscopy pathway (Chapters 07–08); colorectal clinic |
| 🟢 ROUTINE   | Settled thrombosed external haemorrhoid; asymptomatic skin tags                                                                  | Primary care; surgical clinic if recurrent                        |

## The Patient in Front of You — Chapter 02 Mini-Case

> **🧑‍⚕️ THE PATIENT IN FRONT OF YOU**
>
> A 78-year-old nursing-home resident with chronic constipation is brought to the ER after straining on the commode. A 7 cm, beefy-red, oedematous tubular mass protrudes from the anus. The junior doctor calls surgery for “strangulated grade IV haemorrhoids” after failing to push it back. On closer inspection the mucosa shows concentric circular folds and is pink and moist, with no black or grey areas. The abdomen is soft.

**Step 1** **Correct the look-alike.** Concentric rings mean full-thickness rectal prolapse, not prolapsed haemorrhoids (radial grooves).

**Step 2** **Confirm viability and give analgesia.** Pink, moist mucosa and a soft abdomen — reduction is appropriate.

**Step 3** **Reduce osmotically.** Cover with granulated sugar for 15–30 minutes, rinse, and reduce with steady pressure through moist gauze; confirm reduction.

**Step 4** **Prevent recurrence and refer.** Start an osmotic laxative, review constipating drugs, and refer to colorectal surgery for assessment and possible repair (abdominal or perineal approach depending on fitness).

## Board-Style Question — Chapter 02

**A 45-year-old man presents to the ER with a sudden, exquisitely painful bluish lump at the anal verge that appeared 18 hours ago after a long-haul flight. He is afebrile (36.8 °C) and systemically well, with no cellulitis or fluctuance. What is the most appropriate management?**

- **A.** IV broad-spectrum antibiotics and incision and drainage in theatre
- **B.** Bedside removal of the thrombosed vessel and clot (excision) under local anaesthetic
- **C.** Topical hydrocortisone cream for 6 weeks without follow-up
- **D.** Urgent pelvic MRI to exclude a horseshoe fistula

**Answer and explanation**

- ❌ **A** This is a clot, not pus; antibiotics and theatre drainage are not indicated.
- ✅ **B** **Correct.** Onset under 72 hours with severe pain in a well, afebrile patient is an acute thrombosed external haemorrhoid. ASCRS 2024 suggests early excision for selected patients; it gives faster relief and fewer recurrences than conservative care. (The AGA 2026 expert review advises incision and drainage instead — the guidelines differ [U]; whichever is used, the whole clot must be removed. Conservative care is reasonable if pain is already easing or the patient prefers it.)
- ❌ **C** Topical steroids do not resolve a thrombosis, and the AGA advises limiting them to 2 weeks; no follow-up is unsafe.
- ❌ **D** Imaging is unnecessary for a clear clinical diagnosis of external haemorrhoidal thrombosis.

## Ten-Item Rapid Revision Checklist — Chapter 02

- [ ] 01 I examine (inspect, DRE, anoscopy where possible) before diagnosing haemorrhoids.
- [ ] 02 I treat fever plus perianal pain as an abscess until proven otherwise.
- [ ] 03 I know fluctuance may be absent in deep abscesses.
- [ ] 04 I record the onset time of a thrombosed external haemorrhoid in hours.
- [ ] 05 I know ASCRS and AGA differ on excision versus incision for thrombosis — and that the whole clot must be removed.
- [ ] 06 I check prolapse colour before touching it.
- [ ] 07 I can tell concentric rings (rectal prolapse) from radial grooves (haemorrhoids).
- [ ] 08 I can perform an osmotic sugar reduction of a viable, oedematous prolapse.

- [ ] 10 I ask about cirrhosis before anyone bands a “haemorrhoid”.

## Five Most Dangerous Mistakes — Chapter 02

1. ⚠️ Sending a febrile patient with perianal pain home with antibiotics and a cream.
2. ⚠️ Reducing gangrenous prolapsed bowel into the pelvis.
3. ⚠️ Making a small stab into a thrombosed haemorrhoid that leaves clot behind.
4. ⚠️ Calling a persistent pigmented or ulcerated lesion “a thrombosed pile” and missing melanoma or carcinoma.
5. ⚠️ Banding a rectal varix in a patient with portal hypertension.
