## A. Fasting and admission (applies across many guides)

### 1. Clear fluids: "no food or drink / including water for 8 hours" ⏳
**Where:** cholecystectomy, appendectomy, laparoscopic and open inguinal hernia, laparoscopic and open ventral hernia, perianal abscess, pilonidal abscess, thyroidectomy.
**Why:** Current anaesthesia guidance allows **clear fluids (water, clear juice without pulp, black tea or coffee) until 2 hours before** elective surgery. Food is stopped 6 hours before, or 8 hours after a heavy or fatty meal. Long fluid fasts cause thirst, headache, dehydration and low blood sugar, with no safety gain.
**Source:** ASA Practice Guidelines for Preoperative Fasting (2017, updated 2023); ESAIC adult fasting guidance; ERAS Society guidelines.
**Suggested wording:** "Stop eating 6 hours before your operation (8 hours after a heavy or fatty meal). You may drink clear fluids such as water until 2 hours before, unless the hospital tells you otherwise."

### 2. Children: "must stop eating and drinking (including water) for 8 hours" ⏳
**Where:** pediatric hernia.
**Why:** For children the safe limits are shorter than for adults: **clear fluids until 1 hour before, breast milk until 4 hours, formula or light food until 6 hours**. An 8-hour fast from water is a particular risk for small children (dehydration, low blood sugar, distress).
**Source:** ESAIC paediatric fasting guideline (2022); APA/ESPA/ADARPEF consensus (2018).
**Suggested wording:** "Your child may have clear fluids such as water until 1 hour before, breast milk until 4 hours before, and formula milk or a light meal until 6 hours before surgery, unless the hospital gives different instructions."

### 3. "Attend the admission office 8 hours before surgery" for emergency and local-anaesthetic operations ⏳
**Where:** perianal abscess, pilonidal abscess, pediatric hernia, lipoma (local anaesthetic, which *also* says "fasting is not required"), open ventral hernia, cholecystectomy.
**Why:** The guides contradict each other. The wrist ganglion guide says "no need for 8-hour prior admission" for local-anaesthetic cases, while the lipoma guide asks for it. Abscess operations are often done urgently, so "attend 8 hours before" may not apply.
**Decision needed:** what is the actual hospital rule for (a) planned general-anaesthetic cases, (b) local-anaesthetic cases and (c) emergencies? The same wording will then be used in every guide.

### 4. Appendectomy: "Night before surgery" and "fasting for at least 8 hours" ⏳
**Why:** Most appendectomies are emergency operations. The patient is usually already fasting in the emergency department, and there is no "night before". The wording suits a planned operation.
**Suggested wording:** "Appendectomy is usually done as an emergency. Do not eat or drink once your doctor suspects appendicitis; the team will tell you when surgery is planned."

## B. Gallbladder (cholecystectomy guide)

### 5. Silent gallstones: "30–40% develop symptoms within 5 years" ⏳
**Why:** Long-term studies show that people whose gallstones were found by chance develop symptoms at about **1–4% per year**, roughly **10–25% over 10–15 years**. That is why most guidelines advise *not* operating on silent stones in most people. The handout figure is about three times higher.
**Source:** Gracie & Ransohoff, NEJM 1982 (18% over 15 years); EASL Clinical Practice Guidelines on gallstones (2016).
**Suggested wording:** "Most silent gallstones never cause trouble: about 1–4 in 100 people a year develop symptoms. Surgery is usually not needed unless symptoms or complications appear, or your surgeon finds a specific risk."

### 6. "Risks of leaving gallstones untreated" table: population not stated ⏳
**Why:** Figures such as "biliary colic 60–70%" and "acute cholecystitis 20–30%" apply to people who **already had symptoms**, not to silent stones. Next to item 5, readers may apply them to everyone.
**Suggested change:** retitle to "Risks of not treating gallstones *that have already caused pain*", and add a reference for the figures, or keep them and let the doctor confirm the source.

### 7. "Surgery remains the only permanent cure" ⏳
**Why:** This is an absolute claim. It is already flagged in `CLINICAL_REVIEW_QUEUE.md`. The same handout also lists post-cholecystectomy syndrome (5–10%) and ongoing discomfort (up to 10–15%).
**Suggested wording:** "Removing the gallbladder is the most effective long-term treatment for gallstones that cause symptoms."

### 8. "…and never experience gallbladder pain again" ⏳
**Why:** This contradicts the handout's own figures for persistent pain after surgery.
**Suggested wording:** "Most patients recover quickly, return to normal life within days, and no longer have gallstone attacks."

### 9. Pregnancy: "Surgery only when necessary, safest during 2nd trimester" ⏳
**Why:** Current guidance says laparoscopic cholecystectomy **can be performed safely in any trimester** when needed. For symptomatic gallstones in pregnancy, operating is preferred over waiting, because repeated attacks carry risk to mother and baby.
**Source:** SAGES Guidelines for the Use of Laparoscopy During Pregnancy (2017).
**Suggested wording:** "If gallbladder surgery is needed during pregnancy, it can be done safely by laparoscopy in any trimester; your surgeon and obstetrician will plan the best timing together."

### 10. Driving: "after 24 hours" ⏳
**Why:** This is much earlier than the other guides (3–5 days for appendectomy and hernia, 10–14 days for open ventral hernia). The general rule is no driving for at least 24 hours after general anaesthesia, and only once you can do an emergency stop without pain and are off sedating painkillers. Car insurers commonly expect this too.
**Suggested wording, used in every guide:** "Do not drive for at least 24 hours after an anaesthetic. Drive again only when you can do an emergency stop without pain and you are not taking painkillers that make you drowsy, usually after [X] days for this operation."
**Decision needed:** the usual number of days for cholecystectomy.

## C. Appendix (appendectomy guide)

### 11. "There are no reliable long-term alternatives. Antibiotics may temporarily control…" ⏳
**Why:** Large randomised trials show that **antibiotics alone are a recognised option for selected adults with uncomplicated appendicitis**. About 6 in 10 avoid surgery at 5 years, and about 4 in 10 later need an appendectomy. Surgery remains standard, especially for complicated appendicitis (perforation, abscess, appendicolith).
**Source:** CODA trial, NEJM 2020; APPAC 5-year follow-up, JAMA 2018; WSES Jerusalem guidelines (2020).
**Suggested wording:** "For some adults with mild, uncomplicated appendicitis, antibiotics alone may be an option; about 4 in 10 of these patients later need surgery. Appendectomy remains the standard treatment, and is needed for complicated appendicitis. Your surgeon will advise what is safest for you."

## D. Hernia guides

### 12. Open inguinal hernia: "This surgery does not affect urinary or sexual function" ⏳
**Why:** This is an absolute claim. The same handout lists nerve injury (2–6%) and testicular ischaemia or atrophy (<1%), and chronic groin pain can affect intimacy. The laparoscopic guide already says "generally preserves".
**Suggested wording:** "This surgery rarely affects urinary or sexual function. Most patients resume normal activities, including intimacy, once pain settles."

### 13. "Delay pregnancy 6–12 months after repair" (laparoscopic and open inguinal, open ventral) ⏳
**Why:** There is little evidence for a fixed waiting period after *inguinal* repair. For umbilical or ventral hernia in women who plan more pregnancies, many surgeons instead advise **delaying elective repair until after the last pregnancy** where it is safe to do so.
**Source:** European Hernia Society / Americas Hernia Society umbilical and epigastric hernia guideline (2020).
**Decision needed:** keep as the doctor's practice, or reword as "discuss the timing of future pregnancy with your surgeon".

## E. Abscess guide

### 14. Perianal abscess: "Up to 30–50% … may develop an anal fistula later" ⏳
**Why:** The figure is widely quoted, but published rates vary a lot, and some pooled analyses report lower figures (roughly 1 in 6 to 1 in 3).
**Decision needed:** keep 30–50% with a source, or use "about 1 in 3 people or fewer".
